## Answer Key

**1. B.** Bonds broken: 1(432) + 1(154) = 586 kJ. Bonds formed: 2(565) = 1130 kJ. ΔH = 586 − 1130 = −544 kJ. (+544 has the wrong sign; −1130 ignores bond breaking.)

**2. C.** Broken: 432 + 239 = 671 kJ. Formed: 2(427) = 854 kJ. ΔH = 671 − 854 = −183 kJ.

**3. D.** Only one C—H and one Cl—Cl bond break: 413 + 239 = 652 kJ. Formed: one C—Cl + one H—Cl = 339 + 427 = 766 kJ. ΔH = 652 − 766 = −114 kJ.

**4. A.** Broken: 941 + 3(432) = 2237 kJ. Formed: 6 N—H = 6(391) = 2346 kJ. ΔH = 2237 − 2346 = −109 kJ.

**5. C.** Broken: 4(413) + 2(495) = 1652 + 990 = 2642 kJ. Formed: 2(799) + 4(467) = 1598 + 1868 = 3466 kJ. ΔH = 2642 − 3466 = −824 kJ.

**6. D.** Broken: 4(413) + 2(239) + 2(154) = 1652 + 478 + 308 = 2438 kJ. Formed: 2(485) + 2(339) + 2(565) + 2(427) = 970 + 678 + 1130 + 854 = 3632 kJ. ΔH = 2438 − 3632 = −1194 kJ (Example 13.5; the value from enthalpies of formation is −1126 kJ).

**7. B.** D (bond energy per mole of bonds) is always positive. Bond breaking requires energy (endothermic, + terms); bond formation releases energy, handled by the minus sign. The method should be limited to gas-phase reactions because bond energies don't include intermolecular interactions.

**8. A.** Bond energies are averages from several compounds, so ΔH from bond energies only approximates the value from enthalpies of formation, here 2(−271) = −542 kJ.

**9. C.** Pairs in the space between two atoms are bonding pairs. Pairs localized on one atom are lone pairs.

**10. A.** Valence electrons: 4 + 4(7) = 32. Four C—F bonds use 8, leaving 24 electrons = 12 lone pairs (three on each F).

**11. C.** CO₂ has 4 + 2(6) = 16 valence electrons; 16 − 4 = 12 remain. Three lone pairs on each O leaves C with only 4 electrons. The correct structure is O═C═O with two lone pairs on each O, so every atom has an octet.

**12. A.** 4 + 5 + 1 = 10 valence electrons (the −1 charge adds one). C≡N uses 6 electrons, and one lone pair on each atom gives both atoms octets.

**13. D.** 5 + 6 − 1 = 10 valence electrons, giving [:N≡O:]⁺ with one lone pair on each atom (Example 13.6f).

**14. B.** Hydrogen follows the duet rule: it is stable sharing two electrons (a filled 1s shell).

**15. C.** 5 + 4(7) − 1 = 32 valence electrons (the + charge removes one).

**16. B.** No single Lewis structure is correct. NO₃⁻ (24 electrons) is the average of three resonance structures, so all N—O bonds are equivalent and intermediate between single and double. The molecule does not flip between them.

**17. C.** 5 + 2(6) + 1 = 18 valence electrons. After two N—O bonds, 14 remain. Two equivalent resonance structures (the double bond on either O) are needed, so each N—O bond is intermediate.

**18. A.** O₃ (18 electrons) has two equivalent resonance structures, with the double bond on either side. CH₄, H₂O and NH₃ each have one Lewis structure.

**19. B.** Resonance compensates for the LE model's assumption that electrons are localized. The nuclei stay in the same positions, only the electron placement differs. Equivalent structures have the same numbers of single and multiple bonds.

**20. C.** BF₃ has 24 valence electrons. In the preferred structure B has only 6 electrons, so it is electron-deficient. It accepts nitrogen's lone pair to form H₃NBF₃, in which B has an octet.

**21. A.** C, N, O and F (second row) always obey the octet rule. Their 2s and 2p orbitals hold only 8 electrons, so they never exceed it. P, S and Xe can exceed it.

**22. D.** Six S—F bonds use 12 electrons, and the six F octets use the other 36. S has 6 bonding pairs = 12 electrons, so it exceeds the octet rule.

**23. B.** When one of several third-row-or-heavier atoms must exceed the octet, the text places the extra electrons on the central atom. 22 − 4 (bonds) − 12 (terminal lone pairs) = 6 electrons = 3 lone pairs on the central I.

**24. D.** NO has 5 + 6 = 11 valence electrons, an odd number. The LE model is based on electron pairs, so it does not handle odd-electron molecules naturally. (NO₂, with 17 electrons, is another example.)

**25. D.** 4 electron-pair bonds ÷ 6 S—F interactions = 2/3 covalent bond per S—F interaction, plus ionic contributions (S +2, two F at −1 in each resonance structure).

**26. B.** BeCl₂ has 2 + 2(7) = 16 electrons. Two Be—Cl bonds (4 e⁻) plus three lone pairs on each Cl (12 e⁻) use all 16, leaving Be with 4 electrons. Be is electron-deficient.

**27. B.** Formal charge = valence electrons − (lone-pair electrons + ½ shared electrons). S: 6 − (0 + ½·8) = +2. O: 6 − (6 + ½·2) = −1. Check: +2 + 4(−1) = −2, the ion's charge.

**28. A.** S: 6 − (0 + ½·12) = 0. Double-bonded O: 6 − (4 + 2) = 0. Single-bonded O: −1. Sum = 0 + 0 + 0 − 1 − 1 = −2. This structure has minimum formal charges but puts 12 electrons on S. The text notes that whether it or the octet structure is better is still disputed for third-row atoms.

**29. D.** N: 5 − (2 + ½·6) = 0. O: 6 − (2 + ½·6) = +1. Sum = +1, the ion's charge.

**30. A.** Xe: 8 − (2 + ½·6) = +3. Each O (three lone pairs, one bond): 6 − (6 + 1) = −1. Sum = +3 − 3 = 0. Check: 6 + 18 + 2 = 26 electrons.

**31. D.** O═C═O: C = 4 − (0 + 4) = 0 and O = 6 − (4 + 2) = 0. C—O—O arrangements give unreasonable formal charges (e.g., C═O═O gives C −2, central O +2). A negative carbon next to a positive oxygen contradicts oxygen's greater electronegativity.

**32. C.** The text's rules: for second-row atoms, formal charges should be as close to zero as possible, with any negative formal charges on the most electronegative atoms. For third-row or heavier atoms (e.g., sulfate) it says the dispute is unresolved. Formal charges are only estimates. Oxidation states exaggerate charge and are unsuitable for this.

**33. D.** N has 3 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair: a tetrahedral arrangement of pairs. The name comes from the positions of the atoms, so the structure is a trigonal pyramid (bond angle 107°).

**34. A.** Lone pairs (0, 1, 2) increase from CH₄ to H₂O. A lone pair is held near only one nucleus, so it takes more space and squeezes the bonding pairs together.

**35. C.** Xe: 8 + 4(7) = 36 electrons. 36 − 8 − 24 = 4, so 2 lone pairs. Six pairs give an octahedral arrangement, and the lone pairs sit 180° apart, so the structure is square planar. The polar Xe—F bonds cancel, so there is no dipole moment.

**36. B.** SO₂ has 18 electrons. S has one lone pair, one single bond and one double bond (counted as one pair), so 3 effective pairs in a trigonal planar arrangement. The molecule is V-shaped, about 120° (no distortion at 120°). The bond dipoles don't cancel, so it has a dipole moment.

**37. D.** Cl: 28 − 6 (bonds) − 18 (F lone pairs) = 4, so 2 lone pairs on Cl. 5 pairs give a trigonal bipyramid. Three bonded atoms and two lone pairs (equatorial) give a T shape.

**38. A.** 34 − 8 − 24 = 2, so 1 lone pair on S. 5 pairs (4 bonding + 1 lone) in a trigonal bipyramid give a see-saw (AB₄) structure.

**39. D.** PCl₄⁺: 5 + 28 − 1 = 32 electrons, 4 bonding pairs and no lone pairs on P, so tetrahedral. PCl₆⁻: 5 + 42 + 1 = 48 electrons, 6 bonding pairs, so octahedral (Example 13.12).

**40. A.** Multiple bonds count as one effective pair. N in NO₃⁻ has 3 effective pairs, so it is trigonal planar with 120° angles, as observed. Any one resonance structure can be used.

**41. C.** A trigonal bipyramid (five pairs) has two different angles between adjacent pairs, 90° (axial–equatorial) and 120° (equatorial–equatorial). (The two axial pairs are 180° apart, but they are not adjacent.)

**42. B.** VSEPR predicts about 107° (like NH₃), but the measured angle is 94°. This is an example of a simple model having exceptions.
