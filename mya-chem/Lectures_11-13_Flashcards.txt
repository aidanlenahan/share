Photoelectric effect\wd\The ejection of electrons from a metal surface when it is struck by electromagnetic radiation (e.g. ultraviolet light) of sufficiently high frequency; called 'Mystery #2' because it conflicted with classical theory.\ww\Photoelectric observation 1: effect of intensity\wd\Increasing light intensity does NOT increase the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electrons; instead, MORE electrons are emitted.\ww\Photoelectric observation 2: threshold frequency\wd\No electrons are emitted unless the radiation has a frequency above a threshold value ν₀ that is characteristic of the metal, no matter how intense the light is.\ww\Photoelectric observation 3: timing\wd\Electrons are ejected immediately, regardless of how low the intensity of the radiation (as long as ν > ν₀).\ww\Photoelectric observation 4: KE vs frequency\wd\The kinetic energy of the ejected electrons increases linearly with the frequency of the incident radiation (above ν₀).\ww\Threshold frequency (ν₀)\wd\The minimum frequency of light needed to eject an electron from a particular metal; it is characteristic of the metal and equals Φ/h.\ww\Einstein's explanation of the photoelectric effect\wd\Electromagnetic radiation consists of particles (photons), packets of energy E = hν; one photon transfers all its energy to one electron, which is ejected only if that energy exceeds the attractive forces holding it (the work function).\ww\Photon\wd\A particle (quantum, packet) of electromagnetic radiation whose energy is related to its frequency by E = hν.\ww\E = hν\wd\Planck relation: energy of one photon equals Planck's constant (h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s) times its frequency; equivalently E = hc/λ.\ww\Blue vs red photons\wd\Blue light has a higher frequency than red light, so each blue photon has higher energy than each red photon.\ww\Photons below threshold energy\wd\If hν < Φ, no electron is ejected regardless of the intensity of the light.\ww\Photons above threshold energy\wd\If hν > Φ, the excess energy (hν − Φ) appears as kinetic energy of the ejected electron.\ww\Photoelectric energy balance\wd\hν = (1/2)mₑv² + Φ, i.e. hν = KE + Φ, so KE = hν − Φ: photon energy = energy needed to remove the electron + kinetic energy of the electron.\ww\Work function (Φ)\wd\Φ = hν₀; the minimum energy (barrier) an electron must overcome to escape from the metal surface; characteristic of each metal.\ww\Graph of KE vs frequency (photoelectric effect)\wd\A straight line KE = hν − Φ: slope = h (the same for every metal), x-intercept = ν₀ (= Φ/h), extrapolated y-intercept = −Φ (= −hν₀); the intercept depends on the metal.\ww\Classical problem with the Rutherford atom\wd\Classical physics predicts an electrically charged particle moving in a circular path continually loses energy by emitting electromagnetic radiation, so the electron should spiral into the nucleus (the 'death spiral') — atoms would be unstable.\ww\Death spiral of the electron\wd\The classical-mechanics prediction that an orbiting electron radiates energy and spirals into the nucleus; contradicted by the stability of atoms.\ww\Line spectra ('Mystery #3')\wd\Atoms emit (or absorb) light only at specific discrete wavelengths, producing lines rather than a continuous spectrum; not in accord with classical physics. Bohr explained both atomic stability and line spectra.\ww\Continuous spectrum\wd\Spectrum produced by common sources (light bulbs, stars, white light through a prism) containing a continuous range of wavelengths/colors that merge together.\ww\Gas discharge tube\wd\A tube containing a low pressure of a gas to which a high voltage is applied; its emitted light, separated by a prism, gives a line spectrum rather than a continuous one.\ww\Line spectra as element signatures\wd\The number, positions and colors of the lines are unique and characteristic of each element (e.g. H, He, Hg, Ne all differ); diffraction glasses act like a prism to show them.\ww\Emission spectrum\wd\Spectrum of light given off by excited atoms; appears as bright colored lines on a dark background (electrons dropping from higher to lower energy levels).\ww\Absorption spectrum\wd\Spectrum obtained by passing white light through a sample; appears as black (dark) lines on a continuous colored background at the wavelengths the atoms absorb (electrons promoted to higher levels).\ww\Balmer formula\wd\ν = C[1/2² − 1/n²] with n = 3, 4, 5, 6; describes the four visible lines of the hydrogen spectrum.\ww\Rydberg equation\wd\1/λ = R_H(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), with n₁ = 1, 2, … and n₂ = n₁ + 1, n₁ + 2, …; predicts all lines of the hydrogen spectrum, including nonvisible ones.\ww\Rydberg constant (R_H)\wd\R_H = 1.10 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ (more precisely 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹); appears in 1/λ = R_H(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²). Related to the Rydberg energy by R_E = hcR_H.\ww\Rydberg energy (R_E)\wd\R_E = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J = 13.6 eV; the ionization energy of ground-state hydrogen; appears in Eₙ = −R_E/n² and ΔE = R_E(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²).\ww\R_E = hcR_H\wd\Relationship between the Rydberg energy (J) and the Rydberg constant (m⁻¹) via Planck's constant and the speed of light.\ww\Balmer series\wd\Hydrogen lines in the VISIBLE region corresponding to transitions to (emission) or from (absorption) n₁ = 2; wavelengths 656 nm (red, n=3), 486 nm (blue-green, n=4), 434 nm (blue-violet, n=5), 410 nm (violet, n=6).\ww\Lyman series\wd\Hydrogen lines in the ULTRAVIOLET region corresponding to transitions to/from n₁ = 1 (e.g. 121.6, 102.6, 97.3 nm).\ww\Infrared lines of hydrogen\wd\Transitions ending on n₁ = 3 (the Paschen series) and higher levels give lines in the infrared region (e.g. 954.6 nm line on the lecture spectrum).\ww\Balmer line wavelengths (H)\wd\656.3 nm (3→2, red), 486.1 nm (4→2), 434.0 nm (5→2), 410.2 nm (6→2).\ww\Bohr assumption 1\wd\The electron moves in circular orbits about the nucleus with motion described by classical physics.\ww\Bohr assumption 2\wd\Only a fixed set of orbits is allowed and in these orbits no energy is emitted; allowed orbits have angular momentum mₑvr quantized in units of h/2π: mₑvr = n(h/2π) = nħ.\ww\Bohr assumption 3\wd\Electrons can only pass from one allowed orbit to another, accompanied by discrete changes in energy.\ww\Angular momentum (classical)\wd\mvr (mass × velocity × radius) for a particle moving in a circle.\ww\ħ (h-bar)\wd\ħ = h/2π; E(photon) = hν = E₂ − E₁.\ww\Absorption of light (Bohr)\wd\Light is absorbed when an electron jumps from a lower orbit to a higher orbit; the photon energy must equal E₂ − E₁.\ww\E(photon) = hν = E₂ − E₁\wd\The energy (and frequency) of light emitted or absorbed equals the difference between the two orbit energies.\ww\Resonance condition\wd\Energy can ONLY be absorbed or emitted if the frequency of the light corresponds EXACTLY to the difference in energy between the initial and final states (hν = E₂ − E₁); photons of other energies are not absorbed/emitted.\ww\Bohr ΔE for a transition\wd\ΔE = R_E(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), where n₁ is the lower level; ΔE = hν = hc/λ.\ww\Planck relationship linking ΔE and wavelength\wd\ΔE = hν = hc(1/λ), so λ = hc/ΔE.\ww\Energy level diagram of hydrogen\wd\Levels at −R_E (n=1), −R_E/4 (n=2), −R_E/9 (n=3), … converging to 0 at n = ∞; transitions to n=1 are Lyman (UV), to n=2 Balmer (visible), to n=3 infrared.\ww\Hydrogen-like ions\wd\Ions with only one electron, e.g. He⁺ (Z = 2) and Li²⁺ (Z = 3); the Bohr model applies to them.\ww\Bohr model for hydrogen-like ions\wd\rₙ = n²a₀/Z and Eₙ = −R_E(Z²/n²), where Z = atomic number; larger Z gives smaller orbits and more negative energies.\ww\Importance of the Bohr atom\wd\It introduced the idea of quantized energy states for electrons in atoms.\ww\Bohr shortcoming 1\wd\It cannot predict the energy levels and spectra of atoms and ions with more than one electron.\ww\Bohr shortcoming 2\wd\It is a hybrid of classical and nonclassical concepts: quantized angular momentum was introduced to force classical predictions to agree with experiment.\ww\Bohr shortcoming 3\wd\It violates the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, since it assigns an exact position and momentum to the electron at the same time.\ww\Evidence that light behaves like a wave\wd\A diffraction pattern (alternating light and dark bands) forms when light passes through two slits and the waves interfere with each other.\ww\Diffraction pattern (double slit)\wd\Circular waves generated at each slit interfere: constructive interference gives bright lines, destructive interference gives dark regions.\ww\Constructive interference\wd\Waves in phase (crests aligned with crests) add to give a larger amplitude — a bright line in a diffraction pattern.\ww\Destructive interference\wd\Waves out of phase (crest meets trough) cancel — a dark region in a diffraction pattern.\ww\Two types of waves\wd\Traveling waves and standing waves.\ww\Traveling wave\wd\A wave that moves with a given speed (e.g. electromagnetic radiation, ocean waves); in the rope analogy the wave travels toward the post while a marked segment only moves up and down.\ww\Standing wave\wd\A wave (e.g. plucked guitar string) whose crests and troughs stay at fixed positions; segments move up and down, and amplitude at the fixed ends is zero.\ww\Node\wd\A point in a standing wave that undergoes no displacement at all (amplitude zero).\ww\Nodes and energy of a standing wave\wd\The greater the number of nodes, the shorter the wavelength, the higher the frequency, and the higher the energy.\ww\Louis de Broglie (1924) proposal\wd\Small particles of matter may at times display wave-like properties (wave-particle duality of matter).\ww\Wave-particle duality\wd\Both electromagnetic radiation and matter show particle-like and wave-like behavior.\ww\de Broglie's view of the Bohr orbit\wd\Standing waves are examples of quantization; the electron in a Bohr orbit is associated with a circular standing wave.\ww\Why only certain orbits are allowed (de Broglie)\wd\If a fractional number of wavelengths fits the orbit, the wave interferes destructively with itself on successive cycles and is destroyed; only whole numbers of wavelengths survive.\ww\nλ = 2πr\wd\de Broglie condition for a circular standing wave: a whole number of wavelengths equals the circumference of the orbit (n = 1, 2, 3, …).\ww\de Broglie relationship\wd\λ = h/p = h/mv (or h = pλ); Electrons show wave properties, cows do not.\ww\Planck's constant (h)\wd\h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (≈ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s).\ww\Particle described as a wave\wd\Small particles (atoms, electrons) are not mathematical points but spread-out entities in space, so their exact position cannot be known.\ww\Heisenberg uncertainty principle\wd\Wave-particle duality places a fundamental limit on how precisely position and momentum of an object can be known at the same instant: ΔpΔx ≥ h/4π.\ww\ΔpΔx ≥ h/4π\wd\Δp = uncertainty in momentum, Δx = uncertainty in position; the product can never be smaller than h/4π.\ww\When does the uncertainty principle matter?\wd\Only when masses are as small as an electron; then particles become 'fuzzy' and cannot be localized.\ww\Consequence of uncertainty for Bohr orbits\wd\Electrons cannot move in well-defined circular orbits because that would mean exactly known position and momentum; this is a fundamental defect of the Bohr model.\ww\Wave of a single wavelength\wd\Momentum is precisely known (p = h/λ), but the wave is spread out in space so the position is unknown.\ww\Wave packet\wd\Combining waves of different wavelengths localizes a particle (interference pattern), but since each wavelength corresponds to a different momentum, the momentum becomes more uncertain.\ww\Probability and electrons in atoms\wd\Because of the uncertainty principle, for an electron in a fixed energy level we can only know the probability of finding it in certain positions.\ww\Schrödinger's reasoning\wd\If an electron is a wave, its position and movement in space must be described by a wave equation; we can therefore only discuss the probability of finding an electron at a point.\ww\Schrödinger equation status\wd\It cannot be derived; it is regarded as a postulate.
