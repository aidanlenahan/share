Photoelectric effect\wd\The ejection of electrons from a metal surface when it is exposed to electromagnetic radiation (e.g. ultraviolet light) of sufficiently high frequency; it conflicted with classical theory.\ww\Photoelectric observation 1: effect of intensity\wd\Increasing light intensity does NOT increase the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electrons; instead, MORE electrons are emitted.\ww\Photoelectric observation 2: threshold frequency\wd\No electrons are emitted unless the radiation has a frequency above a threshold value ν₀ characteristic of the metal, no matter how intense the light is.\ww\Photoelectric observation 3: timing\wd\Electrons are ejected immediately, regardless of how low the intensity of the radiation (as long as ν > ν₀).\ww\Photoelectric observation 4: KE vs frequency\wd\The kinetic energy of the ejected electrons increases linearly with the frequency of the incident radiation (above ν₀).\ww\Threshold frequency (ν₀)\wd\The minimum frequency of light needed to eject an electron from a particular metal; characteristic of the metal; ν₀ = Φ/h.\ww\Einstein's explanation of the photoelectric effect\wd\Electromagnetic radiation consists of particles (photons), packets of energy E = hν; one photon transfers its energy to one electron, which is ejected only if that energy is enough to overcome the attractive forces holding it (the work function).\ww\Photon\wd\A particle (packet, quantum) of electromagnetic radiation whose energy is related to its frequency by E = hν.\ww\E = hν\wd\Planck relation: the energy of one photon equals Planck's constant times its frequency; since c = λν, also E = hc/λ.\ww\Planck's constant (h)\wd\h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s; the proportionality constant between photon energy and frequency (E = hν).\ww\Speed of light (c)\wd\c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s; relates wavelength and frequency of light by c = λν.\ww\Blue vs red photons\wd\Blue light has a higher frequency than red light, so each blue photon has a higher energy than each red photon.\ww\Photons below the threshold energy\wd\If hν < Φ, no electron is ejected, regardless of the intensity of the light.\ww\Photons above the threshold energy\wd\If hν > Φ, the excess energy (hν − Φ) appears as kinetic energy of the ejected electron.\ww\Photoelectric energy balance\wd\hν = (1/2)mₑv² + Φ, i.e. hν = KE + Φ, so KE = hν − Φ: photon energy = energy needed to free the electron + kinetic energy of the electron.\ww\Work function (Φ)\wd\Φ = hν₀; the minimum energy (barrier) an electron must overcome to escape from the metal surface; characteristic of each metal.\ww\Graph of KE vs frequency (photoelectric effect)\wd\A straight line KE = hν − Φ: slope = h (the same for every metal); x-intercept = ν₀ = Φ/h and extrapolated y-intercept = −Φ, which depend on the metal.\ww\Classical problem with the Rutherford atom\wd\Classical physics predicts that a charged particle moving in a circular path continually loses energy by emitting electromagnetic radiation, so the electron should spiral into the nucleus (the 'death spiral') and atoms would be unstable.\ww\Line spectrum\wd\A spectrum consisting of discrete lines at specific wavelengths rather than a continuous range; atoms exhibit line spectra, which classical physics could not explain.\ww\Continuous spectrum\wd\A spectrum containing a continuous range of wavelengths (colors) that merge together, e.g. white light from a light bulb or star separated by a prism.\ww\Gas discharge tube\wd\A tube containing a low pressure of a gas to which a high voltage is applied; its light, separated by a prism, gives a line spectrum, not a continuous one.\ww\Line spectra as element signatures\wd\The number, positions and colors of the spectral lines are unique and characteristic of each element, so they identify the element.\ww\Emission spectrum\wd\Light given off by excited atoms: bright colored lines on a dark background, produced when electrons drop from higher to lower energy levels.\ww\Absorption spectrum\wd\Obtained by passing continuous light through a sample: black (dark) lines on a continuous background at the wavelengths the atoms absorb (electrons promoted to higher levels).\ww\Emission vs absorption lines of an element\wd\The dark lines in an element's absorption spectrum occur at the same wavelengths as the bright lines in its emission spectrum, because both correspond to the same energy-level differences.\ww\Balmer formula\wd\ν = C(1/2² − 1/n²) with n = 3, 4, 5, 6 and C a constant; fits the four visible lines of the hydrogen spectrum.\ww\Rydberg equation\wd\1/λ = R_H(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), with n₁ = 1, 2, … and n₂ = n₁ + 1, n₁ + 2, …; predicts all lines of the hydrogen spectrum, visible and nonvisible.\ww\Rydberg constant (R_H)\wd\R_H = 1.10 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ (more precisely 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹); used in 1/λ = R_H(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²).\ww\Rydberg energy (R_E)\wd\R_E = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J = 13.6 eV; the ionization energy of ground-state hydrogen; appears in Eₙ = −R_E/n² and ΔE = R_E(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²).\ww\R_E = hcR_H\wd\Relationship between the Rydberg energy (J) and the Rydberg constant (m⁻¹) through Planck's constant and the speed of light.\ww\Balmer series\wd\Hydrogen lines in the VISIBLE region from transitions to (emission) or from (absorption) n₁ = 2; lines at 656 nm (n = 3), 486 nm (n = 4), 434 nm (n = 5) and 410 nm (n = 6).\ww\Lyman series\wd\Hydrogen lines in the ULTRAVIOLET region from transitions to (emission) or from (absorption) n₁ = 1; the highest-energy series.\ww\Infrared lines of hydrogen\wd\Transitions ending on n₁ = 3 (Paschen series) or higher levels give lines in the infrared region (smaller energy gaps).\ww\Bohr assumption 1\wd\The electron moves in circular orbits about the nucleus with motion described by classical physics.\ww\Bohr assumption 2\wd\Only a fixed set of orbits is allowed, and in these orbits no energy is emitted; allowed orbits have angular momentum mₑvr quantized in units of h/2π: mₑvr = n(h/2π) = nħ.\ww\Bohr assumption 3\wd\Electrons can only pass from one allowed orbit to another, and this is accompanied by discrete changes in energy.\ww\Angular momentum\wd\mvr (mass × velocity × radius) for a particle moving in a circle; quantized in the Bohr model.\ww\ħ (h-bar)\wd\ħ = h/2π; the unit in which Bohr quantized angular momentum (mₑvr = nħ).\ww\Principal quantum number (n) in the Bohr model\wd\The integer n = 1, 2, 3, … labeling the allowed orbits; it determines the orbit radius and energy.\ww\Bohr orbit radius\wd\rₙ = ε₀n²h²/(πe²mₑ) = n²a₀; allowed radii grow as n².\ww\Bohr orbit energy\wd\Eₙ = −(e⁴mₑ/8ε₀²h²)(1/n²) = −R_E(1/n²), n = 1, 2, 3, …; energies are negative (bound electron) and approach 0 as n → ∞.\ww\Bohr radius (a₀)\wd\a₀ = 0.529 Å; the radius of the n = 1 Bohr orbit (r₁ = a₀), effectively the radius of the hydrogen atom and the first estimate of the size of atoms.\ww\Zero of energy in the Bohr model\wd\E = 0 corresponds to n = ∞ (electron completely removed from the atom); all bound states have negative energy, the lowest being n = 1 (E₁ = −R_E).\ww\Ionization energy of hydrogen\wd\The energy to remove the electron from the ground state (n = 1 → ∞): R_E = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J = 13.6 eV; from level n it is R_E/n².\ww\Spacing of Bohr energy levels\wd\Energy levels get closer together as the electron moves to higher orbits (larger n).\ww\Emission of light (Bohr)\wd\Light is emitted when an electron jumps from a higher orbit to a lower orbit; E(photon) = hν = E₂ − E₁.\ww\Absorption of light (Bohr)\wd\Light is absorbed when an electron jumps from a lower orbit to a higher orbit; the photon energy must equal E₂ − E₁.\ww\Resonance condition\wd\Energy can only be absorbed or emitted if the frequency of the light corresponds EXACTLY to the energy difference between the initial and final states; photons of other energies are not absorbed or emitted.\ww\Bohr ΔE for a transition\wd\ΔE = R_E(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), where n₁ is the lower level; ΔE = hν = hc/λ, so λ = hc/ΔE.\ww\Energy level diagram of hydrogen\wd\Levels at −R_E (n = 1), −R_E/4 (n = 2), −R_E/9 (n = 3), … converging to 0 at n = ∞; emission to n = 1 is Lyman (UV), to n = 2 Balmer (visible), to n = 3 infrared.\ww\Hydrogen-like ions\wd\Ions with only one electron, e.g. He⁺ (Z = 2) and Li²⁺ (Z = 3); the Bohr model applies to them.\ww\Bohr model for hydrogen-like ions\wd\rₙ = n²a₀/Z and Eₙ = −R_E Z²/n², where Z = atomic number; larger Z gives smaller orbits and more negative energies (transition energies scale by Z²); the ionization energy from n = 1 is Z²R_E.\ww\Importance of the Bohr atom\wd\It introduced the idea of quantized energy states for electrons in atoms and explained both atomic stability and the hydrogen line spectrum.\ww\Bohr shortcoming 1\wd\It cannot predict the energy levels and spectra of atoms and ions with more than one electron.\ww\Bohr shortcoming 2\wd\It is a hybrid of classical and nonclassical concepts: quantized angular momentum was introduced to force the predictions of classical physics to agree with experiment.\ww\Bohr shortcoming 3\wd\It violates the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, because it assigns the electron an exact position and momentum at the same time.
