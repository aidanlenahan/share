Localized electron pair bonding model\wd\Model in which two atoms are held together by a shared pair of electrons; the negative charge of the electron pair acts as the 'glue' holding the positive nuclei together (e.g. H2).\ww\Valence electrons\wd\Electrons in the outer shell of an atom; to a first approximation they alone (not the total number of electrons) determine an element's chemistry and the compounds it forms.\ww\Core electrons\wd\Electrons in the inner shells; they correspond to the previous noble gas configuration and do not participate significantly in chemical reactions.\ww\Shell model of the atom\wd\An atom of nuclear charge n+ has x valence electrons and (n − x) core electrons; its chemistry depends mainly on x.\ww\Core electron counts (noble gas cores)\wd\[He] = 2, [Ne] = 10, [Ar] = 18, [Kr] = 36, [Xe] = 54, [Rn] = 86 core electrons.\ww\Number of valence electrons of main group elements\wd\Equals the 'old' group number (I–VIII) or the last digit of the 'new' group number (1, 2, 13–18). Example: N is old group V / new group 15, so it has 5 valence electrons.\ww\Helium exception (periodic table)\wd\He sits in group 18 (VIII) but has only 2 valence electrons; this can be rectified by an alternative periodic table that places He above Be.\ww\Isoelectronic (valence isoelectronic)\wd\Atoms in the same periodic group have the same number of valence electrons and are called isoelectronic (more properly valence isoelectronic), e.g. N and P, F and Cl. For simple ions it is easy to check the octet (or duet).\ww\Electron count\wd\The number of electrons associated with a given atom in a molecule: its lone pair electrons plus both electrons of every bond it forms.\ww\Line representation of a bond\wd\A line between two atoms represents a shared electron pair (2-center-2-electron covalent bond) to which each atom formally contributes one electron.\ww\Trend CH4 → NH3 → H2O → HF\wd\Moving right from C to N to O to F, the number of bonds decreases (4, 3, 2, 1) and lone pairs emerge (0, 1, 2, 3); every central atom has an octet.\ww\Why boron forms BH3\wd\B has only 3 valence electrons, so it forms 3 bonds to H; BH3 does not have an octet configuration (B has 6 electrons).\ww\Sextet\wd\Six electrons in the valence shell of an atom, as for boron in BH3.\ww\Electronically unsaturated / hypovalent / electron deficient\wd\Terms for a species like BH3 with fewer than 8 electrons on an atom. In ethane each C gets 4 (neutral).\ww\Formal charge\wd\Charge assigned to an atom assuming each bond's electrons are split equally. Formal charges are NOT real charges and do NOT describe the electron distribution; their main purpose is electron bookkeeping. They must be included in Lewis structures.\ww\Formal charge formula\wd\Formal charge = group valence − number of electrons in lone pairs − number of bonds. The electron count on Y decreases by 2; the count on X is unchanged.\ww\Moving a pair when Y has fewer than 8 electrons\wd\Turn a lone pair on X into an extra bond to Y, giving Y⁻=X⁺. The electron count on Y increases by 2; the count on X is unchanged.\ww\Zumdahl method for Lewis structures\wd\(1) Sum the valence electrons from all atoms (only the total matters); (2) use a pair of electrons to form a bond between each pair of bound atoms; (3) arrange the remaining electrons to satisfy the duet rule for H and octet rule for second-row elements (trial and error).\ww\Ozone (O3) Lewis structure\wd\Trial O=O=O gives the central O 10 electrons; take one O–O bond back to a terminal O: O=O⁺–O⁻. All atoms have octets; central O⁺ has 3 bonds + 1 LP, terminal O⁻ has 1 bond + 3 LP.\ww\Allotrope\wd\A different structural form of the same element, e.g. ozone (O3) is an allotrope of oxygen.\ww\Resonance\wd\When a single Lewis structure cannot describe the bonding, the actual structure is a resonance hybrid: a composite or average of two or more Lewis structures, none of which has an independent existence.\ww\Resonance double-headed arrow\wd\The ↔ arrow connects resonance structures. It does NOT mean equilibrium, and resonance does NOT mean rapid oscillation between the forms.\ww\Ozone bond order\wd\The structure O=O⁺–O⁻ suggests O3 is asymmetric, but both O–O bonds are equivalent; each has bond order 1.5.\ww\Bond order\wd\Number of shared electron pairs between two atoms, averaged over resonance structures: single 1, double 2, triple 3; O3 1.5; XeF2 0.5.\ww\Benzene (C6H6)\wd\Classic example of resonance: two equivalent structures with alternating C=C and C–C bonds, but all C–C bond lengths are actually equal (no alternation).\ww\Circle in the benzene ring\wd\Used to show that the bonding cannot be properly represented by a single Lewis structure in which a line represents a shared electron pair.\ww\Resonance structures rule\wd\Resonance structures differ only in the positions of electrons, NOT the positions of nuclei.\ww\Limitation of the line formalism\wd\The 'line' (2-center-2-electron bond) breaks down for some molecules (benzene, O3, hypervalent molecules), but it is still an amazingly useful device for describing many molecules.\ww\Equivalent (degenerate) resonance structures\wd\Resonance structures related by symmetry that have the same energy, e.g. the two benzene structures or the three nitrate structures.\ww\Non-equivalent resonance structures\wd\Not all resonance structures are equivalent (degenerate); some differ in energy, e.g. the BF3 structures with and without B=F⁺.\ww\Sum of formal charges\wd\The formal charges in a Lewis structure add up to the overall charge of the species: 0 for a neutral molecule (e.g. N2O: 0 + 1 − 1), −1 for [NO3]⁻ (+1 − 1 − 1) and −1 for [ClO4]⁻ (+3 + 4 × (−1)).\ww\Where to put the charge in a charged trial structure\wd\On one atom, usually the central atom; it does not matter which one, because moving electron pairs redistributes formal charges automatically.\ww\Breakdown of the octet rule: three situations\wd\1. Hypervalent molecules ('valence shell expansion').\ww\Odd-electron molecules\wd\Any molecule with an odd number of electrons cannot have an octet on every atom, e.g. NO (11 valence electrons) and NO2 (17).\ww\Hypovalent molecule\wd\A molecule in which an atom has less than an octet configuration; BH3 (B has 6 electrons) is an excellent example of a bona fide hypovalent molecule.\ww\BF3 and resonance\wd\Unlike H, F has lone pairs, so one F can donate a lone pair to B in some resonance structures: B⁻=F⁺, giving B 8 electrons.
