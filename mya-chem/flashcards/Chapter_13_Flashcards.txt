Bond energy (D)\wd\Energy required to break one mole of a given bond; D always has a positive sign\ww\ΔH from bond energies\wd\ΔH = ΣD(bonds broken) − ΣD(bonds formed); energy required to break bonds minus energy released when bonds form\ww\Sign of bond breaking\wd\Breaking bonds requires energy (endothermic), so its energy terms are positive\ww\Sign of bond formation\wd\Forming bonds releases energy (exothermic), so its energy terms carry a negative sign\ww\Why bond-energy ΔH is approximate\wd\Bond energies are averages taken from several compounds, so ΔH from bond energies won't exactly match ΔH from enthalpies of formation\ww\Phase limitation of bond-energy ΔH estimates\wd\Use only when all reactants and products are gases; bond energies ignore intermolecular interactions\ww\Localized electron (LE) model\wd\Model assuming a molecule is composed of atoms bound together by using atomic orbitals to share electron pairs, localized on an atom or between two atoms\ww\Lone pairs\wd\Electron pairs localized on a single atom (not involved in bonding)\ww\Bonding pairs\wd\Electron pairs found in the space between two atoms (shared)\ww\Three parts of the LE model\wd\1) Lewis structure for the valence electron arrangement; 2) VSEPR model for geometry; 3) description of the atomic orbitals used to share electrons or hold lone pairs\ww\Lewis structure\wd\Representation of the arrangement of valence electrons among the atoms in a molecule; shows only valence electrons\ww\Basis of Lewis structure rules\wd\In most stable compounds, atoms achieve noble gas electron configurations\ww\Duet rule\wd\Hydrogen forms stable molecules by sharing two electrons, giving it a filled valence shell (He configuration)\ww\Octet rule\wd\Second-row nonmetals (C through F) are stable when surrounded by eight electrons, filling the 2s and three 2p orbitals\ww\Step 1 for writing Lewis structures\wd\Sum the valence electrons from all atoms (only the total matters); add one electron per negative charge, subtract one per positive charge\ww\Step 2 for writing Lewis structures\wd\Use a pair of electrons to form a bond between each pair of bound atoms\ww\Step 3 for writing Lewis structures\wd\Arrange the remaining electrons to satisfy the duet rule for H and the octet rule for second-row elements\ww\Line in a Lewis structure\wd\A line represents a shared (bonding) pair of electrons; dots represent lone pairs\ww\Resonance\wd\Invoked when more than one valid Lewis structure can be written for a molecule; the actual electron structure is the average of the resonance structures\ww\Resonance structures\wd\Valid Lewis structures with the same arrangement of nuclei that differ only in placement of electrons; shown with double-headed arrows\ww\Meaning of the resonance double-headed arrow\wd\The molecule does not flip between structures; the actual structure is an average (superposition) of all of them\ww\Why resonance is needed\wd\The LE model assumes electrons are localized, but electrons are actually delocalized over the molecule; resonance compensates for this defect\ww\Equivalent Lewis structures\wd\Structures containing the same numbers of single and multiple bonds (e.g., the two for O₃); equally important\ww\Nonequivalent Lewis structures\wd\Structures containing different numbers of single and multiple bonds\ww\Electron-deficient atoms\wd\Boron and beryllium often have fewer than eight electrons around them; such compounds are very reactive\ww\Octet rule for C, N, O, F\wd\Second-row elements C, N, O and F should always be assumed to obey the octet rule\ww\Why second-row elements never exceed the octet\wd\Their valence orbitals (2s and 2p) can accommodate only eight electrons\ww\Elements that can exceed the octet rule\wd\Only Period 3 elements and beyond; in the simplest model they use empty valence d orbitals\ww\Placing leftover electrons\wd\Draw single bonds, satisfy octets of all atoms, then place any remaining electrons on atoms with available d orbitals (Period 3 or beyond)\ww\Which atom exceeds the octet when several can\wd\Place the extra electrons on the central atom (e.g., the central I in I₃⁻)\ww\Classical explanation of expanded octets\wd\Third-row atoms use empty 3d orbitals to hold electrons beyond eight\ww\Problem with the d-orbital explanation\wd\Calculations show 3d orbitals are much higher in energy than 3s and 3p, so theorists widely agree d orbitals are not used in bonding\ww\Hyperconjugation\wd\Binding n atoms to a central atom using fewer than n electron pairs around the central atom, preserving the octet rule through resonance structures\ww\Odd-electron molecules\wd\Molecules with an odd number of valence electrons, such as NO (11) and NO₂ (17); the pair-based LE model does not handle them naturally\ww\Formal charge\wd\Formal charge = (valence electrons on free atom) − (valence electrons assigned to the atom in the molecule)\ww\Valence electrons assigned (formal charge)\wd\(Number of lone pair electrons) + ½(number of shared electrons)\ww\Assumptions for assigning electrons in formal charge\wd\Lone pair electrons belong entirely to the atom; shared electrons are divided equally between the two sharing atoms\ww\Sum of formal charges\wd\The sum of formal charges of all atoms must equal the overall charge of the molecule or ion\ww\Formal charge vs oxidation state\wd\Oxidation states assign both shared electrons to the more electronegative atom, exaggerating charge; formal charge splits them equally and is better for evaluating Lewis structures\ww\Best Lewis structure (second-row atoms)\wd\Formal charges as close to zero as possible, with any negative formal charges on the most electronegative atoms\ww\Formal charge and third-row atoms\wd\For molecules or ions with third-row or heavier atoms (e.g., sulfate), which structure is best is still unclear\ww\Formal charge and central atom choice\wd\CO₂ is O=C=O (all formal charges 0) rather than C–O–O, which gives unreasonable charges like negative C next to positive O\ww\Cautions about formal charge\wd\Formal charges are only estimates, not actual atomic charges, and can lead to wrong predictions; experiments decide the true bonding\ww\Molecular structure\wd\The three-dimensional arrangement of the atoms in a molecule\ww\VSEPR model\wd\Valence shell electron-pair repulsion model: the structure around an atom is determined by minimizing electron-pair repulsions, placing bonding and lone pairs as far apart as possible\ww\VSEPR step 1\wd\Draw the Lewis structure for the molecule\ww\VSEPR step 2\wd\Count the electron pairs around the central atom and arrange them to minimize repulsions (as far apart as possible)\ww\VSEPR step 3\wd\Determine the positions of the atoms from the ways the electron pairs are shared\ww\VSEPR step 4\wd\Name the molecular structure from the positions of the atoms, not the electron pairs\ww\2 electron pairs\wd\Linear arrangement, 180° (e.g., BeCl₂, CO₂)\ww\3 electron pairs\wd\Trigonal planar arrangement, 120° (e.g., BF₃)\ww\4 electron pairs\wd\Tetrahedral arrangement, 109.5° (e.g., CH₄); four pairs are always arranged tetrahedrally\ww\5 electron pairs\wd\Trigonal bipyramidal arrangement with 90° and 120° angles (two trigonal pyramids sharing a base)\ww\6 electron pairs\wd\Octahedral arrangement with 90° angles\ww\Four pairs: AB₄ / AB₃ / AB₂\wd\Tetrahedral (CH₄) / trigonal pyramidal (NH₃) / V-shaped or bent (H₂O)\ww\Five pairs: AB₅ / AB₄ / AB₃ / AB₂\wd\Trigonal bipyramidal (PCl₅) / see-saw (SF₄) / T-shaped (ClF₃) / linear (I₃⁻)\ww\Six pairs: AB₆ / AB₄ with 2 lone pairs\wd\Octahedral (SF₆, PCl₆⁻) / square planar (XeF₄, lone pairs 180° apart)\ww\Bond angles CH₄, NH₃, H₂O\wd\109.5°, 107°, 104.5° for 0, 1, 2 lone pairs; the angle decreases as lone pairs increase\ww\Lone pair space requirement\wd\Lone pairs require more room than bonding pairs and compress the angles between bonding pairs\ww\Why lone pairs take more space\wd\A lone pair is held near only one nucleus, while a bonding pair is confined between two nuclei\ww\Placing lone pairs\wd\Choose the arrangement that gives lone pairs the most room; avoid lone pair–lone pair angles of 90°\ww\120° rule for lone pairs\wd\An angle of 120° gives lone pairs enough space, so no distortion; angles less than 120° are distorted when lone pairs are present\ww\Lone pair positions in a trigonal bipyramid\wd\Lone pairs go where they are at least 120° from each other (equatorial), e.g., all three in I₃⁻, making it linear\ww\Multiple bonds in VSEPR\wd\A double or triple bond counts as one effective electron pair\ww\Resonance and VSEPR\wd\When a molecule exhibits resonance, any one of the resonance structures can be used to predict its structure\ww\Molecules with no single central atom\wd\Apply VSEPR to each atom bonded to at least two others (e.g., in CH₃OH, C and O are each tetrahedral, slightly distorted at O)\ww\Polar bonds vs polar molecules\wd\A molecule with polar bonds has no dipole moment if its geometry makes the bond polarities cancel (e.g., XeF₄, CO₂)\ww\VSEPR failure: phosphine (PH₃)\wd\Predicted about 107° like NH₃, but the actual bond angles are 94°; simple models have exceptions
