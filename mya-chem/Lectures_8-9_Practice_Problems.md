# Lectures 8–9 Practice Problems: Drawing rules, resonance, octet exceptions, oxidation numbers, VSEPR and polarity

Choose the single best answer for each question. Answers and explanations are in the Answer Key at the end.

**Formulas used in these lectures**

- Formal charge = valence e⁻ − lone-pair electrons − number of bonds
- Electron count on A = valence e⁻ of A + number of bonds − formal charge
- Nonbonding electrons on A = valence e⁻ of A − number of bonds − formal charge
- Unknown oxidation number = charge on species − Σ(known oxidation numbers)
- Steric number SN = CN (atoms bonded) + LP (lone pairs); VSEPR notation AXₓEₑ with x + e = SN

**Conventions**

- Common valence (bonds needed to fill the valence shell, from XHₙ): H 1, F 1, Cl 1, O 2, N 3, C 4
- Double bond counts as 2 bonds, triple bond as 3 bonds (for electron counts); a multiple bond counts as one domain in VSEPR
- Oxidation numbers: element = 0; F always −1; O usually −2 (not with F or in O–O bonds); H +1 with nonmetals, −1 with metals, B or Si
- SN 2 linear · 3 trigonal planar · 4 tetrahedral · 5 trigonal bipyramidal · 6 octahedral · 7 pentagonal bipyramidal

## Answer Key

**1. C.** Common valence = number of H atoms in the hydride XHₙ. NH₃ gives N a valence of 3 (H 1, F 1, O 2, N 3, C 4).

**2. B.** Formal charge is zero when the atom forms the number of bonds equal to its common valence, e.g. N with 3 bonds, O with 2.

**3. D.** Rule (3): place the charge on the central atom of the trial structure. The terminal atoms are automatically correct with an octet because their bonds were drawn to satisfy their common valence.

**4. C.** 6 − 6 − 1 = −1. This O⁻ motif is isoelectronic with neutral F (1 bond, 3 lone pairs).

**5. A.** Turning a bond pair into a lone pair on X removes 2 electrons from Y's count. X's count is unchanged because the pair stays in X's valence shell (it just changes from bonding to nonbonding).

**6. C.** Y gains a bond, so its count rises by 2 and it becomes Y⁻. X gives up a lone pair to make a bond, so it becomes X⁺, but its count is unchanged.

**7. B.** 16 valence electrons (4 + 6 + 6). The two double bonds use 8, leaving 8 electrons = 4 lone pairs, two on each O. C has no lone pairs.

**8. D.** Moving one P=O bond pair onto O gives P⁺ (8 electrons) and O⁻. All atoms then have octets. The d-orbital explanation is wrong.

**9. A.** O₃ is a hybrid of two equivalent structures, each with one O–O single and one O=O double bond. Average: (1 + 2)/2 = 1.5.

**10. D.** Resonance is not oscillation and ↔ is not an equilibrium arrow. The real molecule is an average (like green from blue and yellow). Resonance structures differ only in electron positions, never nuclei.

**11. A.** All C–C bond lengths in benzene are equal; there is no alternation of single and double bonds. The circle shows that one Lewis structure (with lines as shared pairs) cannot properly represent the bonding.

**12. B.** Resonance structures related by symmetry are EQUIVALENT and have the same energy, i.e. DEGENERATE. Not all resonance structures are equivalent.

**13. C.** N has 4 bonds (one double, two single) and no lone pairs: 5 − 0 − 4 = +1, count 5 + 4 − 1 = 8. Double-bonded O: 6 − 4 − 2 = 0. Each single-bonded O: 6 − 6 − 1 = −1. Sum = +1 + 0 − 2 = −1.

**14. A.** Moving one N≡N pair onto the terminal N gives ⁻N=N⁺=O; moving one N=O pair onto O gives N≡N⁺–O⁻. Both have counts of 8, 8, 8, and the formal charges appear without any calculation.

**15. B.** Nonbonding electrons = valence − bonds − formal charge = 5 − 2 − (−1) = 4 (two lone pairs). Check: count = 4 + 4 = 8.

**16. D.** Count = 7 + 8 − (−1) = 16. Each move lowers Cl by 2: 16 → 14 → 12 → 10 → 8. Four moves give Cl³⁺ with four single bonds and four O⁻.

**17. D.** The three situations are odd-electron molecules, hypovalent (electron-deficient) molecules, and hypervalent molecules ("valence shell expansion").

**18. A.** With an odd number of electrons, not every atom can have an octet. N with 7 and O with 8 is better than N with 8 and O with 9 (more than an octet is bad).

**19. C.** Unlike BH₃ (B: 6 electrons, truly hypovalent), F has lone pairs that can be donated to B in one resonance structure, giving B 8 electrons and F⁺. A positive formal charge on F is acceptable; it is not the real charge.

**20. A.** N is about −0.74 and each H about +0.44. N is negative because it is more electronegative than H, even though its formal charge is +1.

**21. B.** Calculations show there is no real valence shell expansion. The assumption that every A–B bond is a shared pair (2-center-2-electron bond) is what fails. On an exam, read "violates the octet rule" as "appears to violate".

**22. D.** Each structure has one Xe–F bond and one "no bond" Xe⁺ F⁻ interaction, so each Xe–F bond order = (1 + 0)/2 = 0.5. Xe has 8 electrons in each structure.

**23. B.** Formal charge comes from breaking bonds homolytically (each partner gets one electron); oxidation number from breaking them heterolytically, giving the pair to the more electronegative atom. They are two extremes; the real charge is in between.

**24. C.** The calculated charges are H +0.18 and Cl −0.18, intermediate between the formal-charge extreme (equal sharing) and the oxidation-number extreme (100% ionic).

**25. D.** Cl (3.16) is more electronegative than N (3.04), so Cl is −1. N = 0 − 3(−1) = +3. Compare NH₃, where N = 0 − 3(+1) = −3.

**26. A.** O is −2. S = −2 − 4(−2) = −2 + 8 = +6.

**27. B.** O is not −2 when there is an O–O bond. H is +1, so 2(+1) + 2x = 0 gives x = −1.

**28. C.** H is −1 when bonded to a metal (Na is +1). It is +1 when bonded to nonmetals, except B and Si, where it is also −1.

**29. D.** H bonded to Si is assigned −1. Si = 0 − 4(−1) = +4.

**30. B.** F is always −1. O = 0 − 2(−1) = +2. This is the exception to O being −2 (O combined with F).

**31. A.** O is −2: 2x + 7(−2) = −2, so 2x = 12 and x = +6.

**32. C.** Cl is −1, so Fe = 0 − 3(−1) = +3, written as the Roman numeral III. FeCl₂ is iron(II) chloride. Oxidation numbers are also used to balance redox equations.

**33. A.** The structure adopted is the one in which repulsion between the valence electron pairs is minimized. This applies to both bonding pairs and lone pairs.

**34. D.** A multiple bond counts as a single electron pair (domain), so use CN, not bond pairs. CN = 3, LP = 0, SN = 3: AX₃, trigonal planar.

**35. C.** O has 2 atoms bonded (X₂) and 2 lone pairs (E₂): AX₂E₂, SN = 4.

**36. A.** NH₃ is AX₃E, SN = 4, so the VSEPR geometry is a tetrahedron. The lone pair occupies one site, so the atoms form a trigonal pyramid. With lone pairs, the molecular geometry does not match the SN geometry.

**37. C.** A lone pair is larger than a bond pair, so its repulsions are greater. The H–A–H angle decreases to minimize lone pair/bond pair repulsion.

**38. B.** The equatorial site is more spacious, with only two 90° interactions versus three for an axial lone pair. 90° interactions matter more than 120° because they are closer. The shape is see-saw.

**39. D.** Cl has 7 valence electrons: 3 bonds + 2 lone pairs, so AX₃E₂ (SN = 5). Both lone pairs are equatorial, giving a T-shape.

**40. A.** Xe: 8 valence electrons; 2 bonds use 2 of them, leaving 8 − 2 − 0 = 6 nonbonding electrons = 3 lone pairs. SN = 5, AX₂E₃. The three lone pairs take the equatorial sites, so F–Xe–F is linear.

**41. C.** SN = 6 (octahedral). Lone pair/lone pair repulsion is greater than lone pair/bond pair and bond pair/bond pair, so the lone pairs take opposite (180°) sites, leaving a square planar arrangement of atoms.

**42. B.** VSEPR geometry is not sensitive to the Lewis structure chosen. Both have CN = 2 and LP = 1, so SN = 3 (trigonal planar VSEPR geometry) and the molecule is bent.

**43. A.** VSEPR fails in many cases; H₂S's 92° angle is far from the tetrahedral 109.5°. VSEPR is best used as an aide memoire. Molecular orbital theory is more sound but lacks VSEPR's simplicity.

**44. D.** SN 5 is trigonal bipyramidal (e.g. PF₅), with two distinct sites: axial and equatorial. SN 6 is octahedral (SF₆) and SN 7 is pentagonal bipyramidal.

**45. D.** CO₂ is linear, so its two polar C–O bond dipoles oppose each other and cancel. In bent H₂O the bond dipoles do not fully cancel, so water is polar.

**46. B.** Polar molecules align so that the negative end (δ⁻ F) is closest to the positive plate and the positive end (δ⁺ H) faces the negative plate.
