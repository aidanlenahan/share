## Answer Key

**1. B.** Intensity is the number of photons. More photons eject more electrons, but each photon still has the same energy hν, so KE_max = hν − Φ does not change.

**2. C.** One photon gives its energy to one electron. If hν < Φ, no electron is ejected no matter how many photons arrive. Violet photons have higher ν, so hν > Φ and ejection is immediate.

**3. D.** E = hc/λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)(3.00 × 10⁸)/(500 × 10⁻⁹ m) = 3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

**4. A.** Φ = hν₀ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(5.44 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹) = 3.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

**5. B.** E_photon = hc/λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)(3.00 × 10⁸)/(400 × 10⁻⁹) = 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. KE = hν − Φ = 4.9695 × 10⁻¹⁹ − 3.6045 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.36 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. (Keep the unrounded values until the end. Rounding each term first gives 1.365 and a misleading 1.37.) (Option A is the photon energy; it forgets to subtract Φ.)

**6. C.** KE = hν − Φ is a straight line with slope h for every metal. The x-intercept (ν₀) and the y-intercept (−Φ) depend on the metal.

**7. A.** Classical physics says an accelerating (circling) charge radiates energy. The electron would lose energy and spiral into the nucleus (the "death spiral"), so classical atoms would be unstable.

**8. D.** This is an absorption spectrum. The atoms absorb only photons that match their energy gaps, so those wavelengths appear as black lines on the continuous spectrum.

**9. B.** 1/λ = R_H(1/2² − 1/3²) = (1.097 × 10⁷)(0.25 − 0.1111) = (1.097 × 10⁷)(0.13889) = 1.524 × 10⁶ m⁻¹. So λ = 6.56 × 10⁻⁷ m = 656 nm (the red Balmer line).

**10. A.** 1/λ = (1.097 × 10⁷)(1/4 − 1/16) = (1.097 × 10⁷)(0.1875) = 2.057 × 10⁶ m⁻¹, so λ = 486 nm. Balmer lines end on n = 2: 656 (3→2), 486 (4→2), 434 (5→2), 410 (6→2).

**11. C.** ΔE = R_E(1/1² − 1/2²) = (2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸)(0.75) = 1.635 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. λ = hc/ΔE = (1.988 × 10⁻²⁵)/(1.635 × 10⁻¹⁸) = 1.22 × 10⁻⁷ m = 122 nm. Transitions to n = 1 are the Lyman series, which is in the ultraviolet.

**12. D.** rₙ = n²a₀ = 3² × 0.529 Å = 4.76 Å.

**13. C.** Eₙ = −R_E Z²/n². For He⁺, Z = 2 and n = 1, so E₁ = −(2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸)(4)/1 = −8.72 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. Removing the electron (to E = 0) takes 8.72 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.

**14. B.** E₁ = −R_E Z²/n² = −13.6 eV × 3²/1² = −13.6 × 9 = −122 eV.

**15. B.** ΔE(1→2) = (2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸)(1 − 1/4) = 1.635 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. ΔE(1→3) = (2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸)(1 − 1/9) = 1.94 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. Ionization needs 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. The photon energy, 1.80 × 10⁻¹⁸ J, matches none of these, so by the resonance condition it is not absorbed.

**16. D.** The Bohr model successfully explained the hydrogen line spectrum (it reproduced the Rydberg equation). A, B and C are the three shortcomings listed in the lecture.

**17. A.** ΔE = R_E Z²(1/2² − 1/3²) = (2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸)(4)(0.13889) = 1.211 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. λ = hc/ΔE = (1.988 × 10⁻²⁵)/(1.211 × 10⁻¹⁸) = 1.64 × 10⁻⁷ m = 164 nm. Because of the Z² factor, this is exactly 1/4 of hydrogen's 656 nm.

**18. B.** When waves from the two slits arrive out of phase (crest meets trough), they cancel. This destructive interference gives a dark region, while constructive interference gives the bright lines.

**19. D.** More nodes means a shorter wavelength, a higher frequency, and a higher energy. The particle in a box shows the same trend.

**20. A.** If the wavelengths do not fit the circumference a whole number of times, the wave interferes destructively with itself on successive cycles. Only orbits with 2πr = nλ survive. Combining this with Bohr's mₑvr = nh/2π gives λ = h/mₑv.

**21. A.** λ = h/mv = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/[(9.109 × 10⁻³¹)(1.00 × 10⁶)] = 7.27 × 10⁻¹⁰ m (7.27 Å). This is comparable to atomic spacings, so the electron's wave nature can be observed.

**22. C.** λ = h/mv = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/[(0.150)(40.0)] = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/6.00 = 1.10 × 10⁻³⁴ m. This is immeasurably small.

**23. B.** λ = h/mv. Because h ≈ 6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is tiny and macroscopic masses are large, λ is far too small to detect. That is why electrons show wave properties and cows do not.

**24. C.** Diffraction is a wave property. Davisson and Germer (1927) diffracted electrons with a nickel crystal, and G. P. Thomson did the same with Al foil. J. J. Thomson's deflection experiments showed particle behavior.

**25. B.** Diffraction is strong when the wavelength is comparable to the spacing of the "slits." Here those slits are the atoms in the crystal or foil.

**26. D.** p = h/λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/(500 × 10⁻⁹) = 1.33 × 10⁻²⁷ kg·m/s.

**27. A.** Δp ≥ h/(4πΔx) = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/(4π × 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 5.27 × 10⁻²⁵ kg·m/s. Δv = Δp/mₑ = (5.27 × 10⁻²⁵)/(9.109 × 10⁻³¹) = 5.8 × 10⁵ m/s. This is huge, so the electron cannot be pictured on a defined orbit.

**28. C.** Ψ has no physical significance by itself and can be positive or negative. Ψ² is the probability density (Max Born's interpretation).

**29. B.** In HΨ = EΨ, H is the Hamiltonian operator, E is the energy, and Ψ is the wave function.

**30. A.** E₁ = h²/(8mL²) = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)²/[8(9.109 × 10⁻³¹)(1.00 × 10⁻⁹)²] = (4.390 × 10⁻⁶⁷)/(7.287 × 10⁻⁴⁸) = 6.02 × 10⁻²⁰ J. It cannot be 0 J because of the zero point energy.

**31. C.** Eₙ = n²h²/(8mL²), so E₃/E₁ = 3²/1² = 9.

**32. B.** For n = 2, Ψ = (2/L)^½ sin(2πx/L). At x = L/2 this is sin(π) = 0, so Ψ² = 0 and the particle has zero probability of being found at the center in this level.

**33. A.** E = 0 would mean exactly zero momentum (Δp = 0). Then ΔpΔx ≥ h/4π requires an infinite Δx, but the particle is somewhere in the box. So the lowest energy (n = 1) is above zero, and the particle is never at rest.

**34. D.** The rules are l = 0 … n − 1 and mₗ = −l … +l. (3, 2, −2) satisfies both. In A, l cannot equal n. In B, mₗ = 2 is larger than l = 1. In C, mₗ must be 0 when l = 0.

**35. B.** A shell has n² orbitals, so 4² = 16 (4s: 1 + 4p: 3 + 4d: 5 + 4f: 7).

**36. C.** l = 0, 1, …, n − 1 gives l = 0, 1, 2 for n = 3, which are the 3s, 3p, and 3d subshells. 3f would need l = 3, and l cannot exceed n − 1 = 2.

**37. C.** |L| = [l(l + 1)]^½ (h/2π). For d, l = 2, so |L| = √(2 × 3)(h/2π) = √6 (h/2π) ≈ 2.45 h/2π.

**38. A.** L_z = mₗ(h/2π). For p (l = 1), mₗ = +1, 0, −1, so L_z = +h/2π, 0, −h/2π. Option D is for a d orbital, and B is the magnitude |L| for p.

**39. B.** The number of radial nodes increases with n: 1s has 0, 2s has 1, and 3s has 2. In general it is n − l − 1 = 3 − 0 − 1 = 2.

**40. D.** Ψ² for 1s is greatest at the nucleus. The radial distribution 4πr²R² includes the shell volume, and it peaks at r = a₀ (the Bohr radius).

**41. B.** The radial probability is 4πr²R². The r² factor (the shell volume) makes it zero at r = 0, even though Ψ² for an s orbital is nonzero at the nucleus.

**42. A.** For one-electron atoms, Eₙ = −R_E Z²/n² depends only on n. All n = 3 orbitals therefore have the same energy (they are degenerate).

**43. D.** l must be ≤ n − 1. For n = 1, l can only be 0, so there is no 1p orbital. p orbitals first appear at n = 2.

**44. C.** The number of angular nodes equals l. For f orbitals l = 3, so each has 3 angular nodes. f orbitals are important for the lanthanides and actinides (d orbitals are the ones for the transition metals).

**45. B.** E₂ = −R_E Z²/n² = −(2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸)(2²/2²) = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. This is the same energy as the n = 1 level of hydrogen.
