Universal grammar vs. local dialect (Chapter 4 frame)\wd\Chapter 4's organizing question: which parts of the mind are universal grammar, built into all of us by evolution, and which are local dialect, written by experience and culture. The final answer is that they write each other.\ww\Herodotus: Darius, the Greeks, and the Callatians\wd\Darius asked Greeks what he would have to pay them to eat their dead fathers, and Callatians what he would have to pay them to burn theirs; both recoiled in horror. Opposite customs (the local dialect) but the same reason: a deep duty to honor their parents (the universal grammar).\ww\Evolutionary psychology\wd\The study of the evolution of behavior and the mind, using principles of natural selection. It focuses mostly on what makes humans alike.\ww\Natural selection\wd\Inherited traits that better enable an organism to survive and reproduce in a particular environment will (in competition with other variations) most likely be passed on to succeeding generations.\ww\Mutation\wd\A random error in gene replication; one source of the variation selection acts on (new gene combinations at conception are another).\ww\Baby schema\wd\Lorenz's term for features that make faces look babyish: large eyes, round cheeks, high forehead, small nose and mouth.\ww\Convergent adaptation\wd\Separate groups facing the same selective pressure evolve the same trait through different genetic routes. Example: European and East African herders evolved different mutations for lactase persistence.\ww\Overimitation\wd\Faithfully copying a demonstrator's actions, including ones that are clearly unnecessary.\ww\Prepared learning\wd\Evolution makes some associations, such as fears of ancestral dangers, far easier to learn than others.\ww\Glocker et al. (2009) baby-face study\wd\Real infant photos were morphed to be more or less babyish. More babyish faces activated the nucleus accumbens (reward center) more strongly, even in women who had never had children. Because human infants are helpless for years, adults drawn to care for babies raised more surviving children: a cute face is literally rewarding.\ww\Holt-Lunstad et al. (2010) social ties and survival\wd\Across many studies, people with strong social ties were 50% more likely to be alive about 7.5 years later, an effect comparable to quitting smoking. Evidence for the need to belong.\ww\Cacioppo: loneliness as a warning signal\wd\Loneliness evolved as a warning signal, like hunger; for our ancestors, isolation could mean death. The evolutionary explanation for the need to belong.\ww\Fessler et al. (2005) disgust in pregnancy\wd\Women in the first trimester of pregnancy, when immunity is lowest, are more easily disgusted, especially by food. Shows disgust is a disease-avoidance system.\ww\Belyaev farm-fox experiment\wd\Silver foxes were bred only for tameness toward humans, and they soon licked hands. Shows temperament can be selected fast: selection works on behavior, and quickly.\ww\Domestication package (farm-fox experiment)\wd\Traits that were never selected but came along with tameness in Belyaev's foxes: floppy ears, curly tails, piebald (spotted) coats.\ww\Lactase persistence (Tishkoff et al., 2007)\wd\Spread in dairying populations (highest in northwest Europe); European and East African herders evolved different mutations with the same effect. Shows evolution is still happening, convergent adaptation, and nature x nurture: a cultural practice (herding) changed the environment, which selected the genes.\ww\Horner & Whiten (2005) puzzle-box study: design\wd\A demonstrator showed young chimpanzees and 3- to 4-year-old children a two-step way to get a reward from a box: (1) poke a stick into a hole on top (useless; it only hit a false ceiling), (2) open a side door and pull out the reward. The box was either opaque (uselessness hidden) or clear (uselessness obvious).\ww\Horner & Whiten (2005) puzzle-box study: results\wd\Opaque box: chimps and children both copied both steps. Clear box: chimps skipped the useless poke; children kept copying it, even when they thought no one was watching (overimitation).\ww\Why the clear box was essential (Horner & Whiten)\wd\In the opaque box, copying both steps is sensible for anyone. Only when the step is visibly useless can you see who copies faithfully anyway.\ww\Overimitation as a foundation of culture\wd\Overimitation looks inefficient, but it lets skills, rituals, and traditions pass between generations even when no one understands every step.\ww\Two consequences of our shared genetic legacy\wd\(1) Some of the legacy is mismatched with modern life, because the environment changed faster than our genes; (2) beneath cultural differences there may be a universal moral grammar.\ww\Evolutionary mismatch\wd\A tendency that was adaptive for our Stone Age ancestors but fits poorly with modern life, because the environment changed faster than our genes.\ww\Mismatch example: food cravings\wd\Then: sweet and fatty foods were rare and valuable, so craving them aided survival. Now: those foods are everywhere, and the same cravings feed obesity and diabetes.\ww\Mismatch example: fears\wd\Then: snakes, spiders, and heights were real dangers. Now: cars, guns, and electrical outlets kill far more people, yet we rarely fear them.\ww\Cook & Mineka (1989) prepared-fear study: design\wd\Lab-raised rhesus monkeys that had never seen a snake (and did not fear them) watched videos of a monkey reacting with strong fear. The videos were edited so the identical fear reaction appeared directed either at a toy snake or crocodile, or at flowers or a toy rabbit.\ww\Cook & Mineka (1989) prepared-fear study: finding\wd\The watchers learned to fear the toy snake and crocodile but not the flowers or rabbit. They were not copying any fear they saw; they were prepared to learn fears of ancestral dangers (Ohman & Mineka, 2001). Evolution shaped what we learn easily, not only what we know.\ww\Why the video editing was crucial (Cook & Mineka)\wd\Editing kept the model monkey's fear reaction identical across conditions, so the only difference between conditions was the object.\ww\Mikhail (2007) universal moral grammar\wd\Like the grammar of language, people everywhere may share unconscious, intuitive principles for judging actions (e.g., whether harm was intended or a side effect), which they apply confidently even when they cannot explain them.\ww\Curry, Mullins, & Whitehouse (2019)\wd\Ethnographies of 60 societies: seven cooperative behaviors were judged morally good wherever observed, and no society treated any as bad. Cultures weigh them differently (one may put family loyalty above fairness to strangers, another the reverse).\ww\Seven cooperative behaviors judged good in all societies (Curry et al., 2019)\wd\Helping family, helping your group, returning favors, being brave, deferring to superiors, dividing resources fairly, respecting others' property.\ww\Trolley dilemmas: design logic\wd\In each version a runaway trolley will kill five unless one person dies instead. The outcome never changes; only how the one dies changes, so any difference in judgment must come from that.\ww\Switch dilemma\wd\Pull a lever to send the trolley onto a dead-end side track, killing one worker. His death is a side effect: if he jumped clear, the five would still be saved.\ww\Loop dilemma\wd\Pull a lever to send the trolley onto a side track that loops back; one man's body stops it. His death is a means (his body is the brake), but with no personal force: if he jumped clear, the trolley would loop around and kill the five.\ww\Footbridge dilemma\wd\Push a large man off a bridge so his body stops the trolley. His death is a means plus personal force.\ww\Switch vs. Loop comparison\wd\Changes only side effect vs. means. The drop in approval from Switch to Loop reflects harm used as a means.\ww\Loop vs. Footbridge comparison\wd\Changes only personal force. The drop in approval from Loop to Footbridge reflects personal force.\ww\Awad et al. (2020) Moral Machine trolley study: design\wd\About 70,000 people in 42 countries (via MIT's Moral Machine website) judged whether sacrifice was acceptable in the Switch, Loop, and Footbridge dilemmas.\ww\Awad et al. (2020) average approval rates\wd\Percent saying sacrifice is acceptable: Switch 81%, Loop 72%, Footbridge 51%.\ww\Awad et al. (2020): what was universal (the grammar)\wd\In every one of the 42 countries the order was the same: Switch > Loop > Footbridge.\ww\Awad et al. (2020): what varied (the dialect)\wd\The level of approval varied by country; several East Asian countries, for example, were less willing to endorse sacrifice.\ww\Relational mobility\wd\How freely people in a society can form and leave relationships (meet new people, leave groups). It describes the society, not one person. It predicted country variation in Awad et al. (2020).\ww\Where relational mobility is high vs. low\wd\High in the Americas, Europe, and Australia/New Zealand; low in East Asia and the Middle East; lower where there is a history of settled, interdependent farming (rice) and historical threat.\ww\Why low relational mobility lowers Footbridge endorsement\wd\In low-mobility societies, endorsing "push the man" could mark you as cold or dangerous, a reputation you cannot escape, so people were less willing. This held controlling for individualism, GDP, and religiosity.\ww\Claessens et al. (2020) reanalysis of Awad\wd\After accounting for the fact that neighboring countries are not independent data points, relational mobility predicted only Footbridge judgments. The universal ordering was not challenged.\ww\Limitations of Awad et al. (2020)\wd\Self-selected online sample (mostly men, young, and college-educated); hypothetical judgments may not match behavior; country-level correlations do not prove causation.\ww\"We feel the weight of harm" (Footbridge refusals)\wd\About half of people refuse to push in Footbridge (Awad et al., 2020): harming someone with our own hands feels wrong even when it saves lives.\ww\Everett, Pizarro, & Crockett (2016)\wd\Across five studies, people who refused to sacrifice were judged more trustworthy and given more money in a trust game. Moral judgments are social signals: someone who would push the man might treat you as a means to an end.\ww\Bartels & Pizarro (2011)\wd\Preference for sacrifice in trolley dilemmas correlated with psychopathy and Machiavellianism. But a "yes" can come from two places.\ww\Process dissociation (Conway et al., 2018)\wd\Measured concern for outcomes and aversion to harm separately. Psychopathy went with weaker harm aversion, not greater concern for lives; utilitarian philosophers endorsed sacrifice out of genuine concern for the greater good.\ww\Utilitarian philosophers vs. people high in psychopathy on Footbridge\wd\Same answer (push), different reasons: philosophers show concern for the greater good; psychopathy shows weak aversion to causing harm. This does not mean utilitarians are psychopaths.\ww\Cima et al. (2010) psychopathic offenders\wd\Psychopathic offenders make the same moral judgments as others. They know the moral grammar but do not feel its pull (knowing vs. caring).\ww\Bereczkei et al. (2010)\wd\High-Machiavellianism students volunteered for charity publicly but not anonymously; prosocial people behaved the same either way.\ww\White (2014)\wd\Higher psychopathy went with more observed helping and less anonymous helping, explained by lower (self-reported) empathy.\ww\Glaucon's tyrant: "the height of injustice is to seem just without being so"\wd\From Republic, Book II. Fits Bereczkei and White: people high in Machiavellianism or psychopathy act good mainly when seen; reputation matters to them only as a tool. Connects back to the Ring of Gyges.\ww\What evolution built into all of us\wd\Not fixed behaviors but a shared toolkit of mind, which experience and culture then tune: a pull toward baby faces, the need to belong, disgust, prepared fears (snakes and heights, not cars), a taste for sweet and fat, adaptive flexibility, faithful copying, and moral intuitions (the same trolley ranking in 42 countries).\ww\Genes -> us (gene-environment direction)\wd\Over a lifetime, genes shape our minds and the environments we seek out and create.\ww\Environment -> gene expression (gene-environment direction)\wd\Within a lifetime, via epigenetics: experience changes how much a gene is read, without changing the DNA. Example: mother rats' licking (Weaver et al., 2004).\ww\Environment -> which genes spread (gene-environment direction)\wd\Across generations, via natural selection. Example: lactase persistence in herding populations.\ww\Why the milk example is selection, not expression\wd\Dairying did not change how herders' genes were read; it favored people who already carried a variant that kept lactase working, and over generations that variant became common. Epigenetics is a change in expression within one individual's life.\ww\Evershed et al. (2022, Nature): why the milk gene spread\wd\The obvious answer, "milk is a valuable food," was incomplete. The gene's spread matched the timing of famine and disease: for someone starving or sick, milk-induced diarrhea could be deadly, so in crises the gene meant survival.\ww\Evershed et al. (2022): milk use is ancient\wd\Fat residues on ancient pottery show Europeans used milk for about 9,000 years, long before the gene spread.\ww\Evershed et al. (2022): the gene came late\wd\Ancient DNA: the lactase variant stayed rare until about 3,000 years ago, and heavier milk use did not line up with stronger selection.\ww\Evershed et al. (2022): UK Biobank finding\wd\In a large UK Biobank sample of adults, people without the lactase variant drank about as much milk, with little harm. Most adults cope fine.\ww\Lesson of Evershed et al. (2022)\wd\Natural selection often works through the worst times rather than ordinary days. Culture (herding) created the milk; crises decided who could safely live on it.\ww\Plasticity\wd\Neural tissue changes and reorganizes in response to experience. Nature and nurture interact to shape synapses, and brain development does not end in childhood.\ww\Rosenzweig, Krech, and colleagues (1960s) enriched-rat study: design\wd\Rats raised together in cages with daily-changing toys (enriched) vs. rats raised alone in bare cages.\ww\Rosenzweig, Krech, and colleagues (1960s) enriched-rat study: finding\wd\Enriched rats developed a heavier, thicker cortex. Not mostly more neurons but more of everything around them: more dendritic branches and synapses, slightly larger cell bodies, more glia, more tiny blood vessels. Enriched neurons look "bushier."\ww\Enrichment in adult animals\wd\Adult animals respond to enrichment too; used connections strengthen, unused ones fade.\ww\Maguire et al. (2000) taxi-driver study\wd\Licensed London drivers had a larger posterior hippocampus, larger with more years driving. Correlational: people with big hippocampi might choose taxi driving (selection).\ww\Woollett & Maguire (2011) taxi-trainee follow-up\wd\Followed taxi trainees and controls for 3 to 4 years, measuring the same people before and after learning. Only the trainees who passed The Knowledge showed hippocampal growth.\ww\Why the taxi-trainee follow-up is causal evidence\wd\Measuring the same people before and after learning rules out the selection explanation. "Drivers who worked longer had bigger hippocampi" is still only a correlation.\ww\Gene expression\wd\How much a gene is read and used to build its product. Every cell has the same DNA (about 20,000 genes); a brain cell and a skin cell differ in expression.\ww\Methyl tags\wd\Small chemical tags (methyl groups) that attach to the start of a gene and make it harder to read, lowering expression; when they come off, expression rises.\ww\Epigenetics\wd\"On top of" genetics: experience places and removes tags, changing gene expression without changing the genes themselves. Helps explain why identical twins grow more different over time.\ww\Stress-brake gene (NR3C1)\wd\The glucocorticoid receptor gene. Its receptors detect stress hormone (cortisol; corticosterone in rats) and signal the body to shut off the stress response (negative feedback). More expression means more brakes.\ww\Weaver et al. (2004) rat-pup licking study: the chain\wd\High maternal licking removes methyl tags on the pup's stress-brake gene, so expression rises, the pup grows more stress receptors ("brake pedals"), and the adult is calm.\ww\High-licking vs. low-licking mother rats: adult outcomes\wd\High-licking: tags removed, higher expression, more receptors, calm adult (still reacts to danger but recovers faster). Low-licking: tags stay on, lower expression, fewer receptors, anxious adult with strong stress reactions.\ww\Cross-fostering (rat-pup licking study)\wd\Pups swapped at birth grew up like the mother who raised them, not the one who bore them, so the effect is not genetic. Cross-fostering is the only design that separates inherited genes from received care.\ww\Persistence and reversal of the licking effect\wd\It lasts into adulthood, long after the licking stops. It can be reversed: a drug that removes methyl tags made anxious adult rats as calm as the others.\ww\McGowan et al. (2009) human methylation study\wd\People who died by suicide and had been abused as children showed more methylation and lower expression of the same stress-brake gene. Suggestive but small and correlational; the strong evidence comes from rats.\ww\Epigenetics as nature and nurture together\wd\The gene was the same; care changed its expression. Without the gene there would be nothing for experience to regulate, so concluding "genes don't matter" is wrong.\ww\Bucharest Early Intervention Project (Nelson, Zeanah, Fox, 2007): design\wd\Institutionalized Romanian children were randomly assigned to foster care or to remain in the orphanage. Beneath our differences lies a great similarity: our capacity for culture.\ww\Norms\wd\Standards for acceptable, expected behavior.\ww\Individualism\wd\Giving priority to one's own goals over group goals and defining identity in terms of personal attributes.\ww\Collectivism\wd\Giving priority to the goals of one's group and defining identity accordingly.\ww\Self: individualism vs. collectivism\wd\Individualism: independent self (identity from internal, individual traits).
