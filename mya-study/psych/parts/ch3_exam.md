## Answer Key

**1. B.** The guide defines consciousness as subjective experience and states that "its defining feature is experience." Reportability (A) describes full consciousness, a level of consciousness rather than its defining feature.
**2. C.** The problem of other minds is the difficulty of perceiving anyone else's consciousness, which is why you cannot verify that another person sees red as you do. The mind-body problem (A) concerns how the mind relates to the brain and body.
**3. D.** The Turing test measures behavior, not subjective experience, so passing it shows only that a machine can act like a mind. This is the problem of other minds applied to machines.
**4. D.** The cocktail-party phenomenon is the guide's example of selectivity, which includes some objects and leaves others out.
**5. A.** Intentionality means being directed toward an object, so the narrator "is always about something."
**6. C.** The four properties are intentionality, unity, selectivity, and transience. Reportability is not one of them.
**7. C.** Full consciousness means you know your mental state and can report it, as this rider does. Self-consciousness (B) would focus on the self as an object ("Do I look nervous?").
**8. B.** Minimal consciousness is low-level sensory awareness and responsiveness that happens without noticing. In the guide's riding example, it is the body balancing unnoticed.
**9. A.** The EMA finding in the guide is that consciousness is dominated by the immediate environment.
**10. C.** The default network is active when a person has no specific task, as seen with fMRI at rest. It is associated with thinking about the self, social life, the past, and the future.
**11. B.** The design compared prior suppression with simply thinking about the bear. The suppression group later thought about the bear more, which is the rebound effect. All participants rang the bell, so A is not the comparison.
**12. D.** The 2020 meta-analysis confirmed the rebound but found it smaller than the classic graph suggests. It did not fail to replicate (A), and the effect was not larger (B).
**13. A.** Ironic processes occur because the monitoring process that searches for errors can itself produce them. Repression (B) belongs to Freud's theory, which is unsupported.
**14. B.** Ironic effects are most likely under distraction or stress. The guide notes that distracted people trying to relax became more anxious, so D has it backward.
**15. C.** The guide states that instructions about what to do work better than instructions about what to avoid. Options A, B, and D all name the outcome to avoid.
**16. B.** The guide's metaphors are a writers' room with no hidden agenda (cognitive) versus one hidden author with its own agenda (Freudian). Freud's view is not supported by research, so C is wrong.
**17. A.** The guide lists Freudian slips as the evidence offered for the dynamic unconscious and repression.
**18. D.** Pfungst's tests showed that Hans succeeded only when both were true, and he answered even for strangers, which rules out A.
**19. C.** Questioners tensed while Hans tapped and relaxed at the right number, and no one, not even von Osten, knew they were giving the answer away. That rules out deliberate signaling (A).
**20. A.** The Clever Hans effect, in which an observer's unintentional cues shape the behavior being observed, is one reason for double-blind procedures.
**21. C.** Rereading a text before sending it is the guide's example of System 2 acting as editor, checking a draft and catching errors.
**22. B.** Editing takes effort, so the editor skips the check when the person is tired, rushed, or distracted.
**23. D.** The guide states that both systems are continuously active while we are awake and draw on different neural pathways. System 1, not System 2, produces the first drafts (C).
**24. A.** EEG timed the brain's readiness signal, EMG timed the finger movement, and participants reported the clock-dot position when they first felt the wish to move.
**25. B.** The readiness signal began about 535 ms before the movement and the conscious wish about 204 ms before. A reverses the two numbers.
**26. C.** The guide says the timing replicates but its meaning is debated. The findings do not prove that free will is an illusion (B).
**27. B.** Schurger et al. (2021) argue that the early signal may be accumulating random noise and that the real decision comes close to the movement.
**28. A.** "Free won't" is Libet's argument that consciousness may still veto an action already under way.
**29. D.** The thought came first (priority), it fit the action (consistency), and no other cause was obvious (exclusivity) (Wegner & Wheatley, 1999). Option B lists the properties of consciousness.
**30. B.** The hypnagogic state is pre-sleep consciousness with geometric flashes and floating thoughts. The hypnopompic state (A) is post-sleep, when dream imagery lingers after the eyes open.
**31. C.** Stage 2 is marked by sleep spindles and K-complexes. Theta waves (A) mark Stage 1.
**32. A.** K-complexes act as "security guards" that assess sudden, louder sounds and reset the cortex if the sound is harmless. Spindles (B) block minor sounds such as a passing car.
**33. D.** REM shows fast, sawtooth waves that resemble waking beta waves, yet the voluntary muscles are paralyzed (muscle atonia).
**34. A.** Waking from deep sleep brings severe sleep inertia, with 10 to 15 minutes of grogginess. REM sleepers wake quickly alert and remember the dream clearly. B reverses the two.
**35. C.** Deep stages dominate early in the night, REM periods lengthen in later cycles, and stages 3 and 4 largely disappear by about halfway through.
**36. B.** Being woken whenever REM began produced memory problems and excess aggression in both humans and rats within a few days.
**37. C.** Sleep apnea is brief stopping of breathing caused by airway obstruction, which also produces snoring. It is most common in middle-aged men with higher weight and often goes unnoticed by the sleeper.
**38. B.** The guide states that real sleepwalkers walk normally with their eyes open, not with arms out and eyes closed (A).
**39. D.** The six disorders are insomnia, sleep apnea, somnambulism, narcolepsy, sleep paralysis, and sleep terrors. A hypnic jerk is a sensation of dropping as you drift off, listed with the pre-sleep states, not a disorder.
**40. A.** Uncritical acceptance happens because the prefrontal cortex, which supports judgment and checking, is deactivated in REM. The editor is asleep. The amygdala (B) explains emotion, not acceptance.
**41. B.** The guide links dreams' intense emotion to the amygdala being highly active in REM. Visual association areas (A) are activated, not deactivated, in REM.
**42. D.** The five characteristics are intense emotion, illogical thought, fully formed meaningful sensation, uncritical acceptance, and difficulty remembering on waking. Critical judgment is reduced in dreams because the prefrontal cortex is deactivated.
**43. C.** Visual impressions appear in about 20% of congenitally blind people's dreams (versus about 75% for the late blind and 100% for sighted people), and they make up for it with more auditory and tactile content. They also have nightmares in nearly 25% of their dreams, so D is wrong.
**44. D.** Activation-synthesis says random activity comes first and meaning is added afterward. Freud (A) says meaning comes first, in disguise.
**45. A.** Domhoff's neurocognitive theory holds that the default network produces dreams similar to waking life, and the diary data support it.
**46. D.** Threat-simulation theory holds that dreams simulate likely threats so we can practice escape and avoidance. NEXTUP (A) is about exploring weak memory associations.
**47. C.** The Tetris finding supports NEXTUP's claim that REM actively explores memory networks, even for experiences the person cannot consciously recall.
**48. B.** Negative reinforcement is continuing drug use to reduce or eliminate withdrawal symptoms. Positive reinforcement (A) is using because the drug feels good.
**49. C.** Psychological dependence is a strong desire to return to the drug after the physical withdrawal symptoms are gone.
**50. B.** Many soldiers left heroin behind on returning home, which shows that the environment matters.
**51. D.** THC acts on receptors normally activated by anandamide. Alcohol increases GABA, stimulants raise dopamine and norepinephrine, and opiates mimic endorphins, so A, B, and C are mismatched.
**52. A.** Opiates are closely related to endorphins, and flooding the endorphin receptors reduces their effectiveness and may depress endorphin production. Serotonin damage (B) is linked to Ecstasy.
**53. D.** Hallucinogens produce little tolerance or dependence, and overdose deaths are rare. Depressants (A) produce both physical and psychological dependence.
**54. B.** Expectancy theory holds that alcohol's effects can be produced by expectations alone, as when people who only think they drank act uninhibited.
**55. A.** Alcohol myopia narrows attention to whatever is most salient, as in the guide's "crying in the bar" example, where a minor sad thought takes over.
**56. C.** Hypnosis does not improve memory accuracy. It can raise confidence and create false memories. The other three options are effects listed in the guide.
**57. D.** Susceptibility varies greatly between people and is not predictable from personality traits.
**58. A.** Suggestions to see color in a gray image changed activity in color-processing areas, which shows that hypnosis can alter brain activity.
