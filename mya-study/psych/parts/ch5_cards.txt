Unit frame: what the child brings vs. what experience adds\wd\Across the first bond, the growing mind, and the moral sense: what does the child bring, and what does experience add? The answer each time is both: the child brings readiness; the people around the child shape what that readiness becomes.\ww\Attachment\wd\An emotional tie with another person; in young children, shown by seeking closeness to the caregiver and distress on separation (a behavioral definition).\ww\Stranger anxiety\wd\Fear of strangers, beginning by about 8 months, when infants have schemas for familiar faces and are starting to crawl away from safety. A sensorimotor-stage phenomenon.\ww\Four signs of a bond\wd\Proximity seeking (stays near), safe haven (returns when frightened), separation distress (protests when caregiver leaves), and secure base (explores from the caregiver).\ww\Safe haven vs. secure base\wd\Safe haven: the infant retreats to the caregiver when frightened (Harlow's fear test, running to the cloth mother). Secure base: the infant explores from the caregiver and checks back (Harlow's strange room).\ww\"Cupboard love" theory\wd\The Freudian and behaviorist view that infants love the mother because she provides food. Undermined by Lorenz (goslings) and Harlow (monkeys).\ww\Imprinting\wd\The process by which certain animals form strong attachments to the first moving object they see during a brief, early critical period.\ww\Critical period\wd\An optimal period early in life when exposure to certain experiences produces normal development.\ww\Sensitive period\wd\A time when an experience has an especially large effect, but missing it does not close the door forever (the human case).\ww\Contact comfort\wd\Soft body contact as the basis of attachment (Harlow).\ww\Temperament\wd\A biologically based pattern of emotional reactivity.\ww\Basic trust (Erikson)\wd\A sense that the world is predictable and people can be counted on, developed through reliable care.\ww\Lorenz goslings study: design\wd\Divided greylag goose eggs: some hatched under their mother, others in an incubator where the first moving thing they saw was Lorenz.\ww\Lorenz goslings study: finding and meaning\wd\Incubator goslings followed Lorenz everywhere. Goslings feed themselves, so the bond did not come from food. Nature supplies the readiness; experience supplies the target.\ww\Hess ducklings study\wd\Exposed ducklings to a moving, quacking model duck at different ages. Imprinting was strongest about 13 to 16 hours after hatching, then dropped sharply.\ww\Imprinting: later work and the human case\wd\Later work showed imprinting is less rigid (birds can re-imprint). Human babies do not imprint; they attach gradually over months (a sensitive period, not a critical period).\ww\Harlow cloth/wire mother study: design\wd\Infant rhesus monkeys had a bare wire mother and a terry-cloth mother; the bottle was on one or the other.\ww\Harlow cloth/wire mother study: finding\wd\Infants fed by the wire mother fed there, then went back to cling to the cloth mother; some nursed from the wire one without letting go of the cloth one. Contact comfort, not food, built the bond.\ww\Harlow's key logic: the dissociation\wd\Food came from one mother, comfort from the other, and the infants chose comfort. That directly challenges the idea that infants attach to whoever feeds them.\ww\Harlow fear test\wd\A noisy drum-beating toy placed in the cage: infants ran to the cloth mother (never the wire one), calmed, then approached the toy, showing a safe haven. Wire-only infants huddled and rocked.\ww\Harlow strange room test\wd\In an unfamiliar room with the cloth mother, infants explored and checked back; without her, they froze and cried. Shows a secure base: secure attachment makes a baby braver, not clingier.\ww\Harlow total isolation: design\wd\Monkeys raised alone (fed, healthy, no social contact) for 3, 6, or 12 months, then placed with peers.\ww\Harlow total isolation: 3 months vs. 6 to 12 months\wd\3 months: terrified at first but adjusted within weeks. 6 to 12 months: huddled, rocked, avoided contact; lasting damage. As mothers, most were neglectful or abusive (never mothered themselves).\ww\Harlow total isolation: recovery and lesson\wd\Paired with younger, gentle monkeys, many recovered within about 6 months. Food is not enough; timing matters. Such research would not be approved today.\ww\Bowlby: first function of attachment (nature)\wd\The bond keeps babies alive. Babies who stayed near a protector, cried at separation, and ran back when frightened were more likely to survive, so evolution built the need to attach into every baby.\ww\Internal working model (Bowlby)\wd\The bond teaches the baby what to expect (nurture). Reliable comfort teaches "People will help me, and I am worth helping"; unreliable or annoyed responses teach "I can't count on people."\ww\Ainsworth's Strange Situation\wd\A lab test for 1-year-olds, essentially Harlow's strange room adapted for humans, in four scenes: play; stranger in, parent out; alone; reunion.\ww\Strange Situation scene 1: Play\wd\Parent and baby enter a room full of toys; baby plays. Purpose: does the baby use the parent as a secure base?\ww\Strange Situation scene 2: Stranger in, parent out\wd\A kind stranger enters; the parent leaves. Purpose: if the baby still wants the parent, the bond is to that person, not just any adult.\ww\Strange Situation scene 3: Alone\wd\Baby is left entirely alone. Purpose: the most stressful moment; a bond shows when comfort is truly needed.\ww\Strange Situation scene 4: Reunion\wd\The parent returns. The key moment: the baby's reaction defines the attachment style.\ww\Secure attachment\wd\Explores with parent present; may cry when she leaves; seeks her at reunion, calms quickly, returns to play. Linked caregiving: sensitive, responsive.\ww\Avoidant attachment (insecure)\wd\Little visible distress at departure; ignores or turns away at reunion. Heart-rate data suggest stress is present but hidden. Linked caregiving: rebuffs or ignores bids for comfort.\ww\Ambivalent / resistant attachment (insecure)\wd\Very upset at departure; at reunion wants to be held but pushes away, stays angry, hard to soothe. Linked caregiving: inconsistent.\ww\Disorganized attachment (insecure)\wd\No consistent strategy: freezes, or approaches and backs away. Added later by Main and Solomon. Linked caregiving: frightening or frightened caregiver.\ww\Madigan et al. (2023): the first 20,000 Strange Situations\wd\Pooled 20,720 parent-child pairs from 285+ studies: secure 51.6%, disorganized 23.5%, avoidant 14.7%, ambivalent (resistant) 10.2%. Secure is about half, correcting the often-repeated "two-thirds secure" figure from small early studies (Ainsworth's original sample was only a few dozen babies).\ww\Madigan et al. (2023): risk factors\wd\Higher family risk (such as poverty) predicted more avoidant and disorganized attachment; parental mental illness, maltreatment, and foster or institutional care predicted more disorganized attachment.\ww\Madigan et al. (2023): group comparisons\wd\No differences between mothers and fathers or between boys and girls. North American samples differed from some other regions, a reminder that attachment research draws on a narrow slice of the world.\ww\Is attachment style caused by parenting? (the problem)\wd\Nature: a temperamentally irritable baby might become insecure regardless of parenting. Nurture: Ainsworth found sensitive mothers had secure babies, but that is a correlation; easy babies might make mothers look sensitive.\ww\Van den Boom's experiment: design\wd\Selected 100 highly irritable newborns from low-income Dutch families. At about 6 months, half the mothers, chosen at random, received three home visits over three months teaching them to notice, read, and soothe their baby's signals.\ww\Van den Boom's experiment: finding\wd\At 12 months, 62% of babies were secure with training vs. 22% without. Sensitive caregiving causes more secure attachment, even in difficult babies.\ww\Van den Boom: why the conclusion is causal\wd\Random assignment balanced temperament across the groups, so the only systematic difference was the training, turning Ainsworth's correlation into causal evidence. Selecting irritable babies happened before assignment and applied to both groups; it is not what made the conclusion causal.\ww\Sroufe's longitudinal study\wd\Followed children from birth to adulthood: securely attached infants were, on average, more socially competent, better at regulating emotions, and more confident in school.\ww\Hazan & Shaver adult love study\wd\Newspaper readers chose among secure, avoidant, and anxious descriptions of their romantic relationships; proportions resembled Ainsworth's early infant figures, and choices related to recalled relationships with parents.\ww\Attachment and later life: the caution\wd\Links are real but modest. Attachment can shift with divorce, loss, or a new stable relationship. The working model is a starting point, not a life sentence.\ww\Bucharest Early Intervention Project: 2023 follow-up design\wd\136 institutionalized Romanian children (6 to 31 months old) were randomly assigned to high-quality foster care or usual institutional care and followed to ages 16 to 18 (2023 update).\ww\Bucharest Early Intervention Project: 2023 follow-up findings\wd\Foster care produced better cognitive, physical, and brain outcomes and fewer psychiatric symptoms; the largest effects were on IQ and attachment disorders, especially reactive attachment disorder.\ww\Reactive attachment disorder\wd\Failing to seek comfort from caregivers at all; one of the largest effects of foster care in the Romanian (Bucharest) study.\ww\Bucharest project: timing vs. stability\wd\Earlier placement predicted better outcomes, but that advantage weakened by adolescence; by then the strongest factor was stability: children who stayed with their original foster family did best.\ww\Human attachment window (exam point)\wd\Humans do not imprint with a window that slams shut. There is a sensitive period (earlier is better, later is still better than never) plus an ongoing need for a stable, responsive caregiver: the secure base, measured over 18 years.\ww\Piaget's core insight\wd\Children of the same age made the same mistakes, which changed in an orderly way with age. Children do not just know less than adults; they think differently. They are active thinkers, little scientists who build theories, test them, and revise them.\ww\Schema\wd\A concept or framework that organizes and interprets information. Example: "Doggy" = four legs, furry, moves.\ww\Assimilation\wd\Interpreting a new experience in terms of an existing schema. Example: seeing a cow and saying "Doggy!"\ww\Accommodation\wd\Adjusting a schema to fit new information, triggered by disequilibrium when a schema fails. Example: "No, that's a cow; cows are big and say moo." The category splits.\ww\Object permanence\wd\Awareness that things continue to exist when not perceived. Example: pulling off a cloth to find a hidden toy. Key achievement of the sensorimotor stage.\ww\Conservation\wd\The principle that mass, volume, and number stay the same despite changes in form. Example: same milk in a tall, thin glass. Absent in preoperational, achieved in concrete operational.\ww\Egocentrism (Piaget)\wd\The preoperational child's difficulty taking another's point of view. Examples: three mountains task; covering own eyes to "hide."\ww\Theory of mind\wd\People's ideas about their own and others' mental states: what others know, want, and believe. Shown by passing the Sally-Anne false-belief test.\ww\Sensorimotor stage\wd\Birth to about 2. Experiencing the world through senses and actions. Key phenomena: object permanence; stranger anxiety.\ww\Preoperational stage\wd\About 2 to 6 or 7. Representing things with words and images, but no logical operations. Key phenomena: pretend play (banana as telephone); egocentrism; no conservation yet.\ww\Logical operations (Piaget)\wd\Mental actions that can be reversed; lacking in the preoperational stage.\ww\Concrete operational stage\wd\About 7 to 11. Thinking logically about concrete events. Key phenomena: conservation; reversibility (if 8 + 4 = 12, then 12 - 4 = 8).\ww\Reversibility (Piaget)\wd\Mentally undoing an action, such as pouring the milk back or reasoning "if 8 + 4 = 12, then 12 - 4 = 8." A key phenomenon of the concrete operational stage; the preoperational child cannot mentally pour the milk back, so fails conservation.\ww\Formal operational stage\wd\About 12 through adulthood. Reasoning abstractly and hypothetically. Key phenomena: abstract logic; systematic hypothesis testing; potential for mature moral reasoning.\ww\Piaget's stages: ages vs. order\wd\Ages are approximate. Piaget cared about the order, which he believed was the same for every child, not the exact ages.\ww\A-not-B error\wd\Around 8 months babies search for hidden objects, but when a toy is hidden repeatedly under A, then under B in full view, the baby searches under A. Before about 8 months (per Piaget), out of sight is out of mind.\ww\Drawbridge study (1985): design\wd\5-month-olds were habituated to a screen swinging through 180 degrees. A box was placed behind it. Possible event: the screen stopped at the box. Impossible event: it swung all the way through (box secretly removed).\ww\Drawbridge study (1985): finding\wd\Babies looked longer at the impossible event, even though it was the familiar, boring motion, showing early object permanence.\ww\Violation of expectation method\wd\Babies look longer at what surprises them; used in the drawbridge and baby math studies. Requires no reaching, moving beyond Piaget's search task.\ww\Why the drawbridge design is convincing\wd\The impossible event was the familiar motion babies were bored of. If they were just responding to novelty, they should have looked longer at the new, shorter swing. Looking longer at the familiar swing makes sense only if they expected the hidden box to stop it. And no reaching is needed.\ww\Baby math (Wynn, 1992)\wd\5-month-olds saw two dolls placed behind a screen and one removed; they looked longer when two remained (2 - 1 = 2) than when one remained, and similarly for 1 + 1 = 1.\ww\Baby math (Wynn, 1992): debate\wd\True number reasoning or tracking individual objects? Either way, Piaget underestimated infants.\ww\Conservation task: preoperational vs. concrete operational reasoning\wd\Preoperational child says the tall, thin glass has more milk, attending to height and ignoring width, and cannot mentally pour it back. Concrete operational child says "It's the same. You just poured it": reversibility and attention to two dimensions.\ww\Three mountains task and its revision\wd\Young children picked their own view for a doll on the other side (egocentrism). When replaced by a familiar game (hiding a doll from toy policemen), most 3½- to 5-year-olds succeeded.\ww\Formal operations: the black snow question\wd\A 9-year-old asked "If snow were black, what color would a snowman be?" may answer "Snow isn't black!", showing difficulty reasoning hypothetically. Teens can reason from premises (if John is in school, Mary is; John is, so Mary is).\ww\Pendulum problem (Inhelder & Piaget)\wd\Younger children change everything at once; formal-operational teens change one variable at a time while holding others constant: the logic of an experiment.\ww\Sally-Anne false-belief task\wd\Sally puts her marble in the basket and leaves; Anne moves it to the box. Where will Sally look? Most 3-year-olds say "the box" (cannot separate own knowledge from Sally's). Most 4- and 5-year-olds say "the basket."\ww\What passing the false-belief test means\wd\Understanding that beliefs live inside minds, that each person has their own, and that beliefs can be wrong: the foundation for explaining, predicting, and deceiving others.\ww\Theory of mind meta-analysis (178 studies)\wd\Found the false-belief shift in many countries, usually between about 3½ and 4½ years.\ww\Predictors of earlier theory of mind\wd\More siblings, more pretend play, and more talk about feelings and thoughts. Language skill is the strongest predictor. Talking about minds helps children discover them.\ww\Theory of mind in deaf children\wd\Deaf children of hearing, non-fluently-signing parents are often delayed; deaf children of fluent signers are not, pointing to language as the key.\ww\Eye-tracking false-belief studies: status\wd\Studies suggesting 1- and 2-year-olds track false beliefs have proven hard to replicate.\ww\Piaget: what holds up\wd\Children are active thinkers who construct understanding (not miniature adults or blank slates); the sequence of milestones holds across cultures; children think differently, not just less; education by learning by doing and teaching matched to readiness.\ww\Piaget: what needed revising\wd\Development is more continuous than stage-like (number conserved before volume); he underestimated infants because his tasks demanded reaching or explaining; many teens and adults do not consistently reason formally (depends on schooling); he pictured a lone scientist and said little about teaching, language, or culture.\ww\Vygotsky's view of the child\wd\The child is an apprentice. Cognitive development results largely from interaction with members of the child's culture, and language is the key tool. Higher mental skills start between people and only later move inside: thought is internalized conversation.\ww\Inner speech (Vygotsky)\wd\Around age 7, a child's out-loud self-guiding muttering goes silent and becomes inner speech.\ww\Joint attention\wd\Focusing on what another person is focused on; by about 9 to 12 months babies follow gaze and pointing, then point themselves. Needed to learn words.\ww\Imitation of intentions\wd\By about 18 months, infants imitate what an adult intended to do, not just what the adult did.\ww\Social referencing\wd\Reading another's emotional reaction as information.\ww\Visual cliff social referencing study\wd\With the mother looking happy, 74% of 1-year-olds crossed the visual cliff; with the mother looking afraid, none did.\ww\Zone of proximal development (ZPD)\wd\The zone between what a child can do alone and what the child can do with guidance from someone more skilled. Learning happens here: mastered tasks produce no growth; tasks far beyond lead to frustration. What a child can do with help today, she will do alone tomorrow.\ww\Scaffolding\wd\Temporary support removed as the child gains skill.\ww\Block-pyramid scaffolding study\wd\30 children aged 3 to 5 built a pyramid of 21 interlocking blocks; good tutors pointed out pieces, demonstrated, simplified, and withdrew help as competence grew.\ww\Private speech\wd\Children talking to themselves to guide their own behavior; becomes inner speech.\ww\ZPD question: the three rings\wd\Inner circle: what the child does alone. ZPD: what she does with help. Outer: beyond reach even with help. The adult's hint is scaffolding; the child later repeating the hint to herself is private speech.\ww\Piaget vs. Vygotsky on talking to oneself\wd\Piaget: egocentric speech, noise from a child who does not realize listeners differ; should fade and be unrelated to task difficulty. Vygotsky: private speech for self-guidance; should increase on harder tasks and predict better performance.\ww\Private speech evidence\wd\Favors Vygotsky: children use more private speech on challenging tasks, especially in their ZPD, and those using more task-relevant private speech perform better and improve faster.\ww\Conversational turns study (2018): design\wd\Tested the "30-million-word gap" claim (low-income children hear 30 million fewer words by age 3). 4- to 6-year-olds wore recorders for two days; software counted adult words, child words, and conversational turns; children also listened to stories in an fMRI scanner.\ww\Conversational turns study (2018): findings\wd\Turns predicted language scores better than words heard and were linked to Broca's area activity while listening to stories; that activity helped account for the income-language link. Lower-income children with many turns did about as well as higher-income children.\ww\Conversational turns study (2018): cautions\wd\Correlational; and the brain finding concerns activity, not structure.\ww\Piaget vs. Vygotsky: child and engine of growth\wd\Piaget: little scientist, growth from exploring the physical world alone. Vygotsky: apprentice, growth from interacting with more skilled people.\ww\Piaget vs. Vygotsky: role of language\wd\Piaget: language reflects thought that already exists. Vygotsky: language creates thought and becomes inner speech.\ww\Piaget vs. Vygotsky: stages and teaching\wd\Piaget: four universal stages; teachers provide rich materials and wait until the child is ready. Vygotsky: continuous, shaped by culture; teach in the ZPD and scaffold.\ww\Modern synthesis of Piaget and Vygotsky\wd\Children are active thinkers who think best in the company of others. The child brings more than Piaget thought (baby physics, baby math); other people add more than he thought (language, scaffolding, social referencing, culture).\ww\Piaget's John and Henry stories\wd\John, called to dinner, accidentally breaks 15 cups; Henry, sneaking jam, breaks 1 cup. Children under about 7 say John is naughtier (outcome); older children say Henry (intention).\ww\Piaget moral shift 1: realism to relativism\wd\Rules go from fixed laws of nature to changeable agreements. Young marble players insisted rules could never change; older ones knew the group could vote.\ww\Piaget moral shift 2: prescriptions to principles\wd\Grasping the principle behind a rule, such as fairness.\ww\Piaget moral shift 3: outcomes to intentions\wd\Moving from judging acts by consequences to judging by intent. Judging intentions requires theory of mind, so moral development rides on cognitive development.\ww\Barrett et al. (2016): design\wd\Adults in ten societies on six continents judged acts (theft, hitting, poisoning a well) done on purpose or by accident.\ww\Barrett et al. (2016): finding\wd\Every society weighed intent to some degree, but the amount varied widely: large in Western samples, much smaller among the Hadza (Tanzanian hunter-gatherers) and Himba (Namibian herders), who judged accidental well-poisoning nearly as harshly. The weight given to intentions is partly a cultural norm.\ww\The Heinz dilemma\wd\Heinz's wife is dying; a druggist charges $2,000 for a drug that cost $200 to make; Heinz raises $1,000, is refused, and steals it. Kohlberg scored the reasoning, not the yes-or-no answer.\ww\Kohlberg: preconventional level\wd\Before about 9. Self-interest: obey to avoid punishment or gain reward. "He'll get in trouble if his wife dies" / "He'll go to jail."\ww\Kohlberg: conventional level\wd\By early adolescence. Social expectations and order: gain approval, uphold laws. "A good husband protects his wife" / "Stealing is against the law."\ww\Kohlberg: postconventional level\wd\Adolescence and beyond, only some people. Principles above particular laws: basic rights, the value of life. Requires formal operations. "A human life is worth more than a druggist's profit."\ww\Kohlberg's stage structure\wd\Six stages (two per level) in an invariant sequence: no skipping, no going back. In a roughly 20-year follow-up of boys, many never went beyond conventional reasoning.\ww\Kohlberg: the answer never determines the stage\wd\Two people who both say "steal it" can be at different levels: "a good husband is expected to protect his wife; people would think badly of him" is conventional; "human life is worth more than a druggist's profit" is postconventional.\ww\Gilligan's critique of Kohlberg (gender)\wd\Kohlberg's original sample was all male, and scoring ranked an ethic of justice above an ethic of care.\ww\Gilligan critique: current status\wd\Meta-analysis of 113 studies: only small gender differences; both sexes use both justice and care, depending on the dilemma. Care is a legitimate moral voice.\ww\Kohlberg culture criticism\wd\27-country review: early stages nearly everywhere, but postconventional reasoning mainly in urban, educated, Western or middle-class groups. Open question: is collectivist duty-based morality, which scores as "conventional," a lower stage or a different morality?\ww\Kohlberg flexibility criticism\wd\The same person reasons at different levels in different situations; moral reasoning is more like a set of skills than rigid stages.\ww\Kohlberg reasoning vs. action criticism\wd\Kohlberg studied what people say, not what they do. Knowing right does not guarantee doing right; young children act morally before they can explain why.\ww\Hamlin, Wynn, & Bloom (2007) helper/hinderer study\wd\In a puppet show, a helper pushed a climber up a hill and a hinderer pushed it down. 14 of 16 ten-month-olds and all 12 six-month-olds reached for the helper.\ww\Hamlin helper/hinderer: replication status (ManyBabies 4)\wd\Original samples were small and hard to reproduce. ManyBabies 4 (37 labs, 5 continents, 567 infants, preregistered): only 49% chose the helper, chance level. The question remains open; this method may not reveal preferences.\ww\Warneken & Tomasello (2006) helping study\wd\An adult hanging laundry "accidentally" dropped a clothespin out of reach, asked for nothing, offered nothing. 22 of 24 18-month-olds helped, often within seconds.\ww\Warneken & Tomasello: control condition and meaning\wd\When the adult threw the clothespin down on purpose, toddlers did not pick it up. So toddlers read the adult's intention, saw an unmet goal, and helped strangers for no reward, long before they could give reasons. Sturdier than the Hamlin finding. Human-raised chimpanzees also helped in simple tasks.\ww\Moral dumbfounding (Haidt)\wd\Feeling an act is wrong but being unable to give reasons, e.g., a family cooks and eats their dog after it is killed by a car, with no one seeing or harmed; people search for reasons and, when each is ruled out, still say "I just know it's wrong."\ww\Haidt's social intuitionist model\wd\Most moral judgments are fast, automatic intuitions; reasoning usually comes afterward to justify them, like a lawyer for a client rather than a judge weighing evidence. Reasoning still matters, especially when others challenge us.\ww\"The emotional dog and its rational tail"\wd\Haidt's image: intuition (the dog) leads and reasoning (the tail) follows. On this view, Kohlberg had the order backward.\ww\Fast intuition leads, reasons follow (connecting the moral-feeling evidence)\wd\The toddler helping study (with its intentional-drop control), Haidt's moral dumbfounding, and Chapter 3's System 1 all suggest that fast, automatic processes often lead and conscious reasons follow.\ww\First bond: child brings vs. experience adds\wd\Brings: an attachment system that keeps infants near protectors (Bowlby), with contact comfort as the glue (Harlow). Adds: the quality of the bond (Ainsworth's styles); sensitive care makes even irritable babies secure (van den Boom); stable care matters for years (Romania).\ww\Growing mind: child brings vs. experience adds\wd\Brings: an active, theory-building mind (Piaget), with more early knowledge than he thought (baby physics, baby math). Adds: language, scaffolding in the ZPD, social referencing, and culture's tools for thinking (Vygotsky).\ww\Moral sense: child brings vs. experience adds\wd\Brings: early helping and perhaps fast moral intuitions (the infant-preference evidence is now in doubt). Adds: reasons, rules, and a culture's view of what counts as right; reasoning matures (Kohlberg), more than one moral voice (Gilligan), and feeling often leads (Haidt).
