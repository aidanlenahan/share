Consciousness\wd\A person's subjective experience of the world and the mind. Its defining feature is experience.\ww\Phenomenology\wd\The study of how things seem to the conscious person; may be essential for understanding mind and behavior.\ww\Problem of other minds\wd\The fundamental difficulty we have in perceiving the consciousness of others. People can report thoughts and feelings, but there is no definitive way to verify anyone else's inner experience (e.g., whether they see red as you do); we assume other minds resemble ours based on trust, not proof.\ww\Mind-body problem\wd\The issue of how the mind is related to the brain and body.\ww\Dualism (Descartes)\wd\The view that the body is a physical machine but the mind or soul is a separate entity. Most psychologists today instead hold that every thought, perception, or feeling corresponds to specific patterns of brain activity.\ww\Turing test\wd\A test of whether a machine's conversational responses are indistinguishable from a human's. Exam point: it measures behavior (what a machine says), not subjective experience (what, if anything, it feels); passing shows a machine can act like a mind, not that it is conscious. It is the problem of other minds applied to machines.\ww\Artificial intelligence (AI)\wd\Machines that mimic human intelligence and behavior. Whether modern AI can achieve human-like intelligence or consciousness is an open, debated question.\ww\Four basic properties of consciousness\wd\Intentionality (always about something), unity (tells one story), selectivity (leaves things out), transience (never stops).\ww\Intentionality\wd\Property of consciousness: being directed toward an object. The narrator is always about something.\ww\Unity\wd\Property of consciousness: integrating information from the senses into a coherent whole. The narrator tells one story.\ww\Selectivity\wd\Property of consciousness: including some objects but not others. The narrator leaves things out. Illustrated by the cocktail-party phenomenon.\ww\Transience\wd\Property of consciousness: the tendency to change. The narrator never stops.\ww\Cocktail-party phenomenon\wd\Tuning in one message while filtering out others nearby; illustrates the selectivity of consciousness.\ww\Minimal consciousness\wd\Low-level sensory awareness and responsiveness: the mind takes in sensations and may output behavior. Riding example: your body adjusts its balance as the horse moves, without your noticing.\ww\Full consciousness\wd\You know, and can report, your mental state. Riding example: 'Three strides to the fence; sit back and keep my leg on.'\ww\Self-consciousness\wd\Attention is drawn to the self as an object. Riding example: 'Everyone is watching me; do I look nervous?' A curse and a blessing: looking in a mirror leads people to evaluate themselves on deep attributes (honesty) and superficial ones (appearance).\ww\Mirror test\wd\Recognizing oneself in a mirror (humans and some animals) is used as evidence of self-consciousness, but its accuracy is debated.\ww\Ironic errors in sport\wd\"Don't hit it into the water" under pressure may make that outcome more likely. A 2023 review of 24 sport studies was mostly supportive, though many were not true experiments. Instructions about what to do work better than instructions about what to avoid.\ww\Experience sampling / ecological momentary assessment (EMA)\wd\People report what they are thinking at random moments. Finding: consciousness is dominated by the immediate environment.\ww\Daydreaming\wd\A state of consciousness in which a seemingly purposeless flow of thoughts comes to mind.\ww\Default network\wd\Brain areas active when a person has no specific task (seen with fMRI at rest). Associated with thinking about the self, social life, the past, and future possibilities; thought to support the internal narrative we use to make sense of the world.\ww\Mental control\wd\The attempt to change conscious states of mind.\ww\Thought suppression\wd\The conscious avoidance of a thought.\ww\Rebound effect of thought suppression\wd\The tendency of a thought to return to consciousness with greater frequency after it has been suppressed.\ww\White bear study (Wegner et al., 1987)\wd\Participants rang a bell whenever a white bear came to mind. Those who first tried to suppress the thought later thought about the bear more than those simply asked to think about it (rebound effect). Status: a 2020 meta-analysis of 31 studies confirmed the rebound, though it is smaller than the classic graph suggests.\ww\Ironic processes of mental control (Wegner, 1994)\wd\Mental control can produce the opposite of the desired state, because the process that monitors for errors can itself produce them. Most likely under distraction or stress.\ww\Ironic effects under distraction (mood and relaxation)\wd\Distracted people trying to get into a good mood became sadder; distracted people trying to relax became more anxious.\ww\Freudian (dynamic) unconscious\wd\An active system holding a lifetime of hidden memories, deepest instincts and desires, and the inner struggle to control them. Key process: repression; evidence offered: Freudian slips. Metaphor: one hidden author with its own agenda. Not supported by research over the past 100+ years.\ww\Repression\wd\In Freud's theory, the process that removes unacceptable thoughts and memories from consciousness and keeps them in the unconscious.\ww\Cognitive unconscious\wd\The modern view: all the mental processes that give rise to thoughts, choices, emotions, and behavior even though the person does not experience them. A rapid, automatic information processor with many processes (perception, language, memory, emotion) running in parallel. Metaphor: a writers' room with no hidden agenda.\ww\Clever Hans (Pfungst, 1907)\wd\A horse that seemed to do arithmetic by tapping his hoof, even for strangers. Pfungst found Hans succeeded only when the questioner knew the answer and Hans could see the questioner: questioners unknowingly tensed while Hans tapped and relaxed at the right number, and Hans stopped when they relaxed. No one, not even the owner, knew they were giving the answer away.\ww\Clever Hans: what it illustrates\wd\The arithmetic happened in the people, not the horse (cognitive unconscious). It also illustrates the problem of other minds: observers assumed a reasoning mind because the behavior looked like counting.\ww\Clever Hans effect\wd\An observer's unintentional cues influence the behavior being observed. One reason for double-blind procedures.\ww\System 1\wd\Fast, automatic, unconscious processing. Produces quick first drafts of thoughts and actions and navigates daily life efficiently. Narrator metaphor: the writers' room.\ww\System 2\wd\Slow, effortful, conscious processing. Engaged for serious mental effort; can check a draft, catch errors, and override. Narrator metaphor: the narrator acting as editor.\ww\Dual-process theories: key facts\wd\Both systems are continuously active while we are awake and draw on different neural pathways. Editing (System 2) takes effort, so it often does not happen.\ww\When the System 2 editor fails to check\wd\When tired, rushed, or distracted. Checking depends heavily on the prefrontal cortex, which becomes less active in REM sleep. Everyday editing: rereading a text before sending, catching a habitual wrong turn, double-checking a quick exam guess.\ww\Libet et al. (1983): design\wd\Participants moved a finger whenever they chose while watching a dot circle a clock face and reported the dot's position when they first felt the wish to move. EEG timed brain activity; EMG timed the movement.\ww\Libet et al. (1983): findings\wd\EEG readiness signal begins about 535 ms before the finger moves; the conscious wish is reported about 204 ms before; movement (EMG) at 0 ms. The narrator may announce a decision the brain is already making.\ww\Libet: current status\wd\The timing replicates; the meaning is debated. Many neuroscientists still hold the classic view. The findings do not prove free will is an illusion; they show our sense of when we decide is not a reliable guide to what the brain is doing.\ww\Accumulator view (Schurger et al., 2021)\wd\Alternative interpretation of Libet: the early signal may be random neural noise building up, and the actual decision may come close to the movement.\ww\'Free won't' (Libet)\wd\Libet's argument that consciousness may still veto an action already under way.\ww\Apparent mental causation (Wegner & Wheatley, 1999)\wd\We conclude a thought caused an action when the thought comes first (priority), fits the action (consistency), and no other cause is obvious (exclusivity).\ww\Who authors the script? (Ch. 3 exam point)\wd\Not one hidden schemer (Freud) but a writers' room of fast, automatic processes you never experience (System 1). The narrator reads the script aloud, takes credit, and sometimes grabs the red pen (System 2). Evidence: Clever Hans, Libet, apparent mental causation.\ww\Unconsciousness (in sleep)\wd\A state sleep can produce in which the mind and brain turn off the functions that create experience.\ww\Altered state of consciousness\wd\A form of experience that departs significantly from the normal subjective experience of the world and the mind; dreaming is one.\ww\Hypnagogic state\wd\Pre-sleep consciousness, halfway between awake and asleep: brief flashes of geometric shapes, floating thoughts that don't quite make sense. Corresponds to Stage 1.\ww\Hypnic jerk\wd\A sudden quiver or sensation of dropping, as if missing a step on a staircase, as you drift off.\ww\Hypnopompic state\wd\Post-sleep consciousness: eyes open, but dream imagery lingers and reality is briefly hard to separate from the dream.\ww\Circadian rhythm\wd\A naturally occurring 24-hour cycle. People in time-free environments still keep a roughly 24-hour rest-activity cycle; the rhythm appears even in insects, plants, and bacteria.\ww\REM sleep\wd\A stage of sleep with rapid eye movements and a high level of brain activity; dreaming occurs most often here. Called paradoxical sleep: fast sawtooth EEG like waking beta, vivid emotional narrative dreams, voluntary muscles paralyzed (muscle atonia). Woken: quickly alert with clear dream memory.\ww\Five stages of sleep (EEG)\wd\Stage 1: theta. Stage 2: sleep spindles and K-complexes. Stages 3 and 4: delta. REM: fast sawtooth waves like waking beta.\ww\Beta and alpha waves\wd\Awake: beta waves (high frequency, alert). Alpha waves appear when relaxed and drowsy.\ww\Stage 1 sleep\wd\Light, transitional sleep; theta waves (lower frequency). Feels like the hypnagogic state: drifting, random visual flashes, floating. Woken: instantly alert; you may insist you were never asleep.\ww\Stage 2 sleep\wd\True, stable sleep; sleep spindles and K-complexes. Awareness of the room is gone; heart rate and temperature drop. Woken: wake fairly easily with a brief moment of confusion.\ww\Stages 3 and 4 sleep\wd\Deep, slow-wave sleep; delta waves (lowest frequency, large and synchronized). Oblivious; physical repair, tissue growth, immune recovery; no narrative dreaming. Woken: severe sleep inertia, groggy and disoriented for 10 to 15 minutes.\ww\Sleep spindles\wd\Stage 2 'noise-cancelers': 1- to 2-second bursts that block minor background sounds (a passing car) from reaching consciousness.\ww\K-complexes\wd\Stage 2 'security guards': large, sharp spikes that assess sudden, louder sounds (a barking dog); if harmless, they reset the cortex to a calm state so you stay asleep.\ww\Waking from Stage 4 vs REM\wd\Stage 4: severe sleep inertia, groggy and disoriented 10 to 15 minutes. REM: brain near waking pace, so quickly alert with clear memory of the dream.\ww\Sleep across the night (hypnogram)\wd\Deep stages dominate early; REM periods grow longer in later cycles; Stages 3 and 4 largely disappear by about halfway through the night.\ww\Sleep and memory consolidation\wd\Sleep after learning appears essential for memory consolidation. Extreme sleep loss can be fatal.\ww\Effects of sleep deprivation\wd\Reduced mental acuity and reaction time; increased irritability, depression, and risk of accidents and injury.\ww\Glymphatic system\wd\Clears potentially neurotoxic waste from the brain and distributes glucose, lipids, and amino acids; the brain may need sleep's reduced activity to perform this daily maintenance.\ww\REM deprivation (Ellman et al., 1991)\wd\Memory problems and excess aggression appear in humans and rats after only a few days of being woken whenever REM begins.\ww\Six sleep disorders\wd\Insomnia, sleep apnea, somnambulism (sleepwalking), narcolepsy, sleep paralysis, sleep terrors (night terrors).\ww\Insomnia\wd\Difficulty falling asleep or staying asleep. About 12% of adults meet criteria in a given month; can stem from lifestyle (night shifts) or from depression, anxiety, or other conditions.\ww\Sleep apnea\wd\The person stops breathing for brief periods while asleep. Caused by airway obstruction, which also produces snoring; most common in middle-aged men with higher weight; often unnoticed by the sleeper.\ww\Somnambulism (sleepwalking)\wd\Arising and walking around while asleep. Real sleepwalkers walk normally, eyes open, sometimes with a glassy stare (not arms out, eyes closed).\ww\Narcolepsy\wd\Sudden sleep attacks in the middle of waking activities.\ww\Sleep paralysis\wd\Waking up unable to move. Sometimes associated with narcolepsy.\ww\Sleep terrors (night terrors)\wd\Abrupt awakenings with panic and intense emotional arousal.\ww\Five characteristics of dream consciousness\wd\Intense emotion, illogical thought, fully formed meaningful sensation, uncritical acceptance, difficulty remembering on waking.\ww\Intense emotion (dreams)\wd\Heart-pounding terror while falling or euphoria while flying; the amygdala is highly active in REM.\ww\Illogical thought (dreams)\wd\Your school hallway becomes a spaceship and your boss is also your grandmother; the story continues anyway.\ww\Fully formed, meaningful sensation (dreams)\wd\You see the water, hear the waves, feel the sand, and taste the salt.\ww\Uncritical acceptance (dreams)\wd\You fly by flapping your arms and never think 'this is impossible.' Explained by prefrontal cortex deactivation in REM: the editor is asleep.\ww\Difficulty remembering dreams\wd\A vivid dream evaporates within seconds of getting up.\ww\The brain in REM\wd\Activated: amygdala, visual association areas, motor cortex, brainstem. Deactivated: prefrontal cortex (planning, judgment, checking). Explains why dreams feel emotional and illogical and why we accept them: the editor is asleep.\ww\Dreaming blind (Meaidi et al., 2014)\wd\Visual impressions appear in 100% of sighted people's dreams, about 75% for the late blind, and 20% for the congenitally blind, who make up for it with more auditory and tactile content. Congenitally blind people have nightmares in nearly 25% of their dreams.\ww\Freud's dream theory\wd\Dreams represent wishes or suppressed thoughts; they begin with meaning (meaning first, disguised). Not supported scientifically.\ww\Activation-synthesis theory\wd\Dreams arise when the brain tries to make sense of random neural activity during sleep; they begin randomly and meaning is added as the mind interprets.\ww\Neurocognitive theory of dreaming (Domhoff)\wd\Dreaming is enabled by the default network (which supports imagination and daydreaming while awake), producing scenarios similar to waking life. Evidence: content analysis of tens of thousands of dream diaries shows 70-80% of dreams dramatize ordinary waking concerns; children under 5 rarely dream vividly, tracking their slowly developing imagination.\ww\Threat-simulation theory\wd\Dreams simulate likely threats so we can practice escape and avoidance (rehearsal for danger).\ww\NEXTUP (Stickgold & Zadra, 2021)\wd\Network Exploration to Understand Possibilities: in REM the brain explores its memory network for weak, non-obvious associations and tests what-if scenarios to extract new meaning. Dreams are an offline data-optimization program, not noise. Evidence: people who play Tetris before bed dream of falling blocks, even amnesic patients who cannot remember playing.\ww\Dream theories: where meaning comes from\wd\Freud: meaning first, disguised. Activation-synthesis: random activity first, meaning added. Neurocognitive: continues waking concerns via the default network. Threat simulation: rehearsal for danger. NEXTUP: active exploration of weak memory associations.\ww\Psychoactive drugs\wd\Chemicals that influence consciousness or behavior by altering the brain's chemical message system.\ww\Positive reinforcement (drug use)\wd\Repeating drug use because it induces a positive psychological state (using because it feels good).\ww\Negative reinforcement (drug use)\wd\Continuing drug use to reduce or eliminate withdrawal symptoms (using to remove withdrawal).\ww\Drug tolerance\wd\Larger doses are required over time to achieve the same effect.\ww\Physical dependence\wd\Pain, convulsions, hallucinations, or other unpleasant symptoms accompanying withdrawal.\ww\Psychological dependence\wd\A strong desire to return to the drug even after physical withdrawal symptoms are gone.\ww\Gateway drug\wd\A drug whose use increases the risk of later use of more harmful drugs; marijuana, alcohol, and tobacco are considered gateway drugs.\ww\Vietnam heroin study (Robins et al., 1980)\wd\Many soldiers who became addicted to heroin in Vietnam left the habit behind on returning home: the environment matters.\ww\Depressants\wd\Reduce CNS activity; sedating; induce sleep at high doses; can stop breathing at extreme doses. Examples: alcohol, barbiturates, benzodiazepines, toxic inhalants. Physical and psychological dependence. Alcohol increases GABA (inhibitory).\ww\Stimulants\wd\Excite the CNS; raise dopamine and norepinephrine. Examples: caffeine, amphetamines, nicotine, cocaine, modafinil, Ecstasy. Confidence and agitated drive; withdrawal brings fatigue and negative emotions. Cocaine: insomnia, depression, aggression, paranoia; death by heart attack or hyperthermia. Ecstasy: empathy and closeness, but jaw clenching, overheating, and possible damage to serotonin neurons.\ww\Narcotics (opiates)\wd\Relieve pain; well-being and relaxation, or stupor and lethargy. Examples: heroin, morphine, methadone, codeine. Closely related to the brain's endorphins; flooding the receptors reduces their effectiveness and may depress endorphin production, so withdrawal is severe.\ww\Hallucinogens\wd\Alter sensation and perception; visual and auditory hallucinations. Examples: LSD, mescaline, psilocybin, PCP, ketamine. Little tolerance or dependence; overdose deaths rare; emotions range from bliss to terror.\ww\Marijuana (cannabis)\wd\Mildly hallucinogenic intoxication from THC, from the leaves and buds of the hemp plant. Impairs judgment, short-term memory, and coordination. Little tolerance or physical withdrawal; psychological dependence possible.\ww\THC and anandamide\wd\THC acts on receptors normally activated by anandamide, which is involved in mood, memory, appetite, and pain.\ww\Drug classes and neurotransmitter links\wd\Alcohol (depressant) increases GABA; stimulants raise dopamine and norepinephrine; opiates mimic endorphins; THC acts on anandamide receptors.\ww\Nicotine and negative reinforcement\wd\Nicotine use is driven far more by the unpleasantness of quitting than by the pleasure of using; the 'relaxation' smokers report is mostly relief from withdrawal.\ww\Expectancy theory (alcohol)\wd\Alcohol's effects can be produced by people's expectations of how alcohol will affect them in a situation. Example: people who think they drank alcohol act more uninhibited even if they did not.\ww\Alcohol myopia\wd\Alcohol hampers attention, so people respond in simple ways to complex situations, reacting to whatever is most salient. Example: 'crying in the bar,' a minor sad thought takes over because it cannot be balanced against others.\ww\Expectancy theory and alcohol myopia: shared prediction\wd\Both predict extreme behavior; alcohol contributes to accidents, aggression, and sexual and intimate partner violence. The same person may become loud, weepy, sullen, or giddy in different circumstances.\ww\Hypnosis\wd\A social interaction in which one person (the hypnotist) makes suggestions that lead to a change in another person's subjective experience of the world. Its essence is leading people to expect that certain things will happen outside their conscious will (e.g., 'your arms are getting heavy').\ww\Hypnotic susceptibility\wd\Varies greatly between people and is not predictable from personality traits.\ww\Posthypnotic amnesia\wd\Failure to retrieve memories following hypnotic suggestions to forget.\ww\Hypnotic analgesia\wd\Reduction of pain through hypnosis in people who are susceptible.\ww\Hypnosis and memory\wd\Hypnosis does not improve accuracy; it can increase confidence and create false memories, so hypnotically refreshed testimony is treated with caution.\ww\Hypnosis color study (Kosslyn et al., 2000)\wd\Suggestions to see color in a gray image changed activity in color-processing areas of the brain.\ww\Involuntariness (hypnosis)\wd\Hypnotized participants feel their actions are happening on their own.\ww\Consciousness as narrator (Ch. 3 frame)\wd\Conscious experience feels complete, accurate, and in control, but it is private (other minds), partial (selectivity), and often reports decisions other processes are already making (System 1, Libet). It goes quiet in deep sleep, runs without an editor in dreams (prefrontal deactivation in REM), is changed by chemicals, and under hypnosis someone else suggests the lines.
