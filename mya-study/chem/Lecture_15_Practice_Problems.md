## Answer Key

**1. B.** After 3p is filled, the next subshell is 4s (n + l = 4), not 3d (n + l = 5), so K is [Ar]4s¹ and Ca is [Ar]4s². At this point 4s is below 3d in energy.

**2. A.** The 3s and 3p orbitals penetrate the [1s²2s²2p⁶] core well, so they feel the rising nuclear charge and drop in energy quickly. The unoccupied 3d orbital does not penetrate, so its energy stays almost the same as in hydrogen up to K (Z = 19).

**3. C.** The 4s principal maximum lies beyond the 3d maximum, so 4s electrons do little to shield 3d. Each added proton therefore raises Zeff(3d) by almost one unit (K → Ca → Sc), and once the transition series is reached the 3d energy drops rapidly.

**4. D.** All 3d orbitals have the same radial dependence, so no 3d electron sits consistently inside another to shield it. The 4s orbital lies outside 3d, so the d electrons shield the increasing nuclear charge from the 4s electrons across the series.

**5. C.** The usual pattern would give 3d⁴4s², but Cr is 3d⁵4s¹: a half-filled 3d subshell with six unpaired, parallel spins.

**6. A.** Cu is 3d¹⁰4s¹ (a filled 3d subshell) rather than 3d⁹4s².

**7. D.** The favorable exchange energy of half-filled (d⁵) and filled (d¹⁰) shells is the usual explanation: electron–electron repulsion between parallel spins is less than between opposed spins.

**8. A.** Three different patterns in one group show that configurations of neutral transition metal atoms depend on a subtle balance of nd and (n+1)s orbital energies and d/d, s/s and s/d electron–electron repulsions. The ndⁿ(n+1)s¹ pattern is more common in the second and third series and is not limited to half-filled shells.

**9. B.** Transition metal cations are 3dⁿ: the electrons are removed from 4s first. Fe (3d⁶4s²) loses both 4s electrons to give Fe²⁺ = [Ar]3d⁶.

**10. D.** Fe³⁺ loses both 4s electrons and one 3d electron: [Ar]3d⁵. By Hund's rule the five d electrons are all unpaired.

**11. A.** Cu (3d¹⁰4s¹) loses the 4s electron and then one 3d electron: Cu²⁺ = [Ar]3d⁹.

**12. B.** Ionization increases Zeff, stabilizing both 3d and 4s, but 3d much more because the stabilization goes roughly as Zeff² and 4s lies outside 3d. The 3d–4s gap becomes too big for moving an electron into 4s to pay off in reduced repulsion, so the ions are 3dⁿ.

**13. C.** Mn²⁺ = [Ar]3d⁵ (both 4s electrons lost). Five d electrons in five d orbitals are all unpaired.

**14. D.** Cr (24 e⁻) → Cr³⁺ has 21 electrons: [Ar]3d³. Fe³⁺ is 3d⁵, Ti²⁺ is 3d², and Sc³⁺ is [Ar] (3d⁰).

**15. A.** A shell has n subshells (4s, 4p, 4d, 4f), n² orbitals (16) and holds 2n² electrons (32), since each orbital holds two electrons with opposite spins.

**16. B.** Down a group the outer electrons are in a shell with a larger n (compare the Bohr radius rₙ = n²a₀/Z), and the Pauli principle keeps the added electrons from occupying the already-filled core.

**17. A.** Across a period electrons enter the same shell, but Zeff increases, so the outer electrons are pulled closer to the nucleus.

**18. D.** K is in period 4 (n = 4 valence shell), below Na, so it is largest. Within period 3, radius decreases Na > Mg > Cl.

**19. C.** Across a row, Zeff increases and the radius decreases, so IE generally increases. Down a group, the radius increases while Zeff hardly changes, so IE decreases.

**20. D.** Be's electron comes from 2s; B's comes from 2p. The np orbitals do not penetrate as well as ns, so the 2p electron feels a lower Zeff and is easier to remove. The same happens for Al versus Mg.

**21. A.** N (2p³) has three unpaired 2p electrons. In O (2p⁴) one 2p orbital is doubly occupied, and the extra repulsion makes one of those electrons easier to remove.

**22. B.** Mg ionizes from 3s; Al ionizes from 3p, which penetrates less, has a lower Zeff and is easier to remove. So IE(Mg) > IE(Al) even though Al has the larger nuclear charge.

**23. C.** In this convention, EA is the energy change for adding an electron. Negative means energy is released and X⁻ is stable. Positive means X⁻ is unstable. (Some texts use the opposite sign convention.)

**24. A.** The halogens' valence np orbital has a high Zeff, and the added electron completes the octet (ns²np⁶), so energy is strongly released.

**25. D.** The noble gases have a filled valence shell (1s² for He, ns²np⁶ for Ne and Ar), so the added electron must go into the next shell, where Zeff is low. It feels mainly electron–electron repulsion, so the EA is positive and the anion is unstable.

**26. B.** Be and Mg have filled ns² subshells, so an added electron must go into the new np subshell, which is higher in energy and penetrates less.

**27. A.** The shared-electron-pair model plus the octet rule explains why C, N, O and F form CH₄, NH₃, H₂O and HF. It fails for O₂'s magnetism, bond strengths and electron-deficient B₂H₆.

**28. C.** The Lewis structure of O₂ has an O=O double bond with every electron paired, which predicts O₂ to be diamagnetic. O₂ is actually paramagnetic (it has unpaired electrons), a key failure of the Lewis model.

**29. B.** Paramagnetic substances are attracted into a magnetic field: liquid oxygen poured between the poles of a magnet is held there. Liquid nitrogen, which is diamagnetic, just pours through.

**30. D.** Resonance structures, such as those of ozone and benzene, have the nuclei in the same positions and differ only in where the electrons are placed. Needing several structures for one molecule is itself a shortcoming of the Lewis model.

**31. C.** B₂H₆ has 2(3) + 6(1) = 12 valence electrons, but eight 2-center-2-electron bonds would need 16. There are too few electrons for a valid Lewis structure, so Lewis theory cannot explain the molecule.

**32. A.** Xe in XeF₂ is surrounded by two bonding pairs and three lone pairs: 10 electrons. The 2-center-2-electron bond picture forces the idea of hypervalent compounds and expansion of the octet.

**33. C.** In VB theory a covalent bond is created by the overlap of orbitals on two atoms. The bonding electron density concentrated in the overlap region is the "glue" holding the nuclei together.

**34. D.** A σ bond (e.g. 1s–1s in H₂, or end-on pz–pz in N₂) has no node along the bond axis and is cylindrically symmetric, so viewed down the axis it looks like an s orbital.

**35. B.** In H₂ the electron density is concentrated between the two nuclei, but it is still highest at the nuclei themselves.

**36. A.** Perpendicular p orbitals overlap side by side to give a π bond: two lobes of electron density, one on each side of the bond axis, with a node along the axis (no density directly between the nuclei). Viewed down the axis it looks like a p orbital, and it is still a single bond.

**37. C.** Bond strength depends on overlap: the greater the overlap, the stronger the bond. Side-by-side (π) overlap is less than end-on (σ) overlap.

**38. B.** Each N has three singly occupied 2p orbitals. The two 2pz orbitals (along the bond axis) overlap end-on to give the σ bond, and the perpendicular 2px and 2py pairs overlap side by side to give two π bonds.

**39. D.** H–C is a single (σ) bond, and C≡N is a triple bond (1 σ + 2 π). Total: 2 σ and 2 π.
