## Answer Key

**1. B.** A pattern of bright and dark lines is an interference (diffraction) pattern, which only waves produce. The circular waves from each slit add or cancel depending on where they meet.

**2. D.** Where waves from the two slits arrive out of phase (crest meets trough) they cancel: destructive interference gives the dark regions. In-phase waves add (constructive interference) to give the bright lines.

**3. A.** A traveling wave (light, an ocean wave) moves with a given speed. In a standing wave (a plucked guitar string) the crests, troughs and nodes stay at fixed positions, and the amplitude at the fixed ends is zero.

**4. C.** More nodes means more half-wavelengths fit in the same length, so λ is shorter, the frequency is higher and the energy of the standing wave is higher.

**5. B.** De Broglie proposed that small particles of matter can behave like waves, with λ = h/p = h/mv. He linked this to the electron in a Bohr orbit acting as a circular standing wave.

**6. D.** nλ = 2πr and mₑvr = nh/2π, so 2πr = n(h/mₑv). Then nλ = n(h/mₑv), giving λ = h/mₑv = h/p.

**7. A.** For the circular standing wave to survive, the circumference 2πr must hold a whole number of wavelengths (nλ = 2πr). Fractional wavelengths interfere destructively with themselves on successive trips around and destroy the wave, so only certain orbits (and energies) exist.

**8. C.** nλ = 2πr, so the n = 3 orbit holds exactly 3 wavelengths.

**9. D.** λ = h/mv = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/[(9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg)(1.00 × 10⁶ m/s)] = 7.27 × 10⁻¹⁰ m (0.727 nm). 1.37 × 10⁹ is mv/h (inverted), and 6.04 × 10⁻⁵⁸ multiplies instead of dividing.

**10. A.** λ = h/mv = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/[(0.145 kg)(30.0 m/s)] = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/4.35 = 1.52 × 10⁻³⁴ m. 4.57 × 10⁻³³ m leaves out the velocity; 2.21 × 10⁻³⁵ m leaves out the mass.

**11. B.** Because h = 6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is so small, a macroscopic mass gives a wavelength (about 10⁻³⁴ m for a baseball) far too small to measure. Wave properties are only noticeable for very small masses such as electrons.

**12. C.** v = h/mλ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/[(9.109 × 10⁻³¹)(1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ m)] = 7.27 × 10⁶ m/s. (0.100 nm = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ m; using 10⁻⁷ m gives 7.27 × 10³ m/s.)

**13. D.** p = h/λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)/(5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m) = 1.33 × 10⁻²⁷ kg·m/s. 3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ is the photon's energy in joules (hc/λ), not its momentum.

**14. C.** Diffraction needs a wavelength comparable to the spacing of the scattering objects. The wavelength of accelerated electrons (like that of X-rays) is similar to the distance between atoms in a crystal or foil, so they diffract.

**15. A.** Deflection by electric and magnetic fields and the measurement of the mass-to-charge ratio (J. J. Thomson) treat the electron as a particle. Diffraction, interference and standing waves are wave behavior.

**16. C.** Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π, where Δx and Δp are the uncertainties in position and momentum. The more precisely one is known, the less precisely the other can be known at the same instant. It only matters for very small masses such as electrons.

**17. A.** Short wavelength means high frequency, high energy and large momentum (p = h/λ). A photon like that knocks the electron and changes its motion, so measuring the position precisely makes the momentum uncertain.

**18. D.** Longer-wavelength photons carry less momentum, so they disturb the electron's momentum less, but a long wavelength cannot pin down the position as precisely. You can't win on both.

**19. B.** Δp ≥ h/(4πΔx) = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/(4π × 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 5.27 × 10⁻²⁵ kg·m/s. Δv = Δp/m = (5.27 × 10⁻²⁵)/(9.109 × 10⁻³¹) = 5.8 × 10⁵ m/s. That is huge, so the electron cannot be localized in a definite orbit. (7.3 × 10⁶ uses h instead of h/4π; 5.3 × 10⁻²⁵ is Δp, not Δv.)

**20. D.** Δp ≥ h/(4πΔx) = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)/(4π × 1.0 × 10⁻³) = 5.27 × 10⁻³² kg·m/s. Δv = 5.27 × 10⁻³²/0.145 = 3.6 × 10⁻³¹ m/s, far too small to matter, so a baseball follows a well-defined trajectory. (4.6 × 10⁻³⁰ uses h instead of h/4π; 5.3 × 10⁻³² is Δp.)

**21. A.** A single wavelength gives a precisely known momentum (p = h/λ), but the wave is spread out through space. Adding waves of different wavelengths localizes the particle, but each wavelength has a different momentum, so the momentum becomes uncertain.

**22. C.** A circular orbit means the electron's position and momentum are both well defined at every moment, which the uncertainty principle forbids. For an atom in a fixed energy level, only the probability of finding the electron at a given position can be known.

**23. A.** H is the Hamiltonian operator, E is the energy and Ψ (psi) is the wave function. The solutions of this differential equation are the wave functions and their energies.

**24. D.** Ψ itself has no physical significance and may be positive or negative, but it contains the information for all properties of the wave. Ψ² is the probability density for finding the electron at a point (Born's interpretation).

**25. B.** The Schrödinger equation cannot be derived; it is treated as a postulate. Solving it gives the allowed wave functions and energies, and it describes only probabilities, not trajectories.

**26. C.** Eₙ ∝ n², so E₃/E₁ = 3²/1² = 9.

**27. A.** Eₙ = n²h²/8mL², so E ∝ 1/L². Halving L multiplies every energy by 4. A longer box or a heavier particle lowers the energies, and n cannot be 0.

**28. D.** E₁ = h²/8mL² = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)²/[8(9.109 × 10⁻³¹)(1.00 × 10⁻⁹ m)²] = (4.390 × 10⁻⁶⁷)/(7.287 × 10⁻⁴⁸) = 6.02 × 10⁻²⁰ J. 6.02 × 10⁻²⁹ J forgets to square L; 2.41 × 10⁻¹⁹ J is E₂.

**29. B.** ΔE = E₂ − E₁ = (4 − 1)E₁ = 3(6.02 × 10⁻²⁰) = 1.81 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. λ = hc/ΔE = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)(3.00 × 10⁸)/(1.81 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 1.10 × 10⁻⁶ m = 1.10 × 10³ nm. (3.30 × 10³ nm uses E₁ alone; 825 nm uses E₂ alone.)

**30. C.** The n = 2 wave function has a node at the center, so Ψ² = 0 there: the particle has zero probability of being at the center in this level.

**31. A.** For n = 1, Ψ² has a single maximum at the center of the box and falls to zero at the walls.

**32. D.** The wave function for level n has n − 1 nodes between the walls, so n = 4 has 3. Energy increases with the number of nodes, just as for a guitar string.

**33. B.** If E were zero, the momentum would be exactly zero (Δp = 0), but the particle is somewhere in the box, so Δx is finite and Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π would be violated. The lowest energy is the zero-point energy (ZPE): a particle in a potential well is never at rest.
