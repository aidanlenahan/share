## Answer Key

**1. B.** Total nodes = n − 1 = 3. Angular nodes = l = 2. Radial nodes = n − l − 1 = 4 − 2 − 1 = 1.

**2. D.** One angular node means l = 1 (a p orbital). Radial nodes = n − l − 1 = 2, so n = 4: a 4p orbital. 3p has 1 radial node; 4d and 3d have 2 angular nodes.

**3. A.** Radial nodes = n − l − 1 = 5 − 0 − 1 = 4. An s orbital has no angular nodes, so all n − 1 nodes are radial.

**4. D.** Radial nodes = n − l − 1. For 3d: 3 − 2 − 1 = 0. 3s has 2, 3p has 1, 4p has 2.

**5. C.** Total = n − 1 = 4; angular = l = 3; radial = 5 − 3 − 1 = 1.

**6. A.** For a given n, the average distance of the electron from the nucleus decreases in the order ns > np > nd. This can be misleading for polyelectron atoms, where penetration of the core matters more.

**7. C.** For a hydrogen-like ion in its 1s state, IE = Z²R_E = (2²)(2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J) = 8.72 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. 4.36 × 10⁻¹⁸ J uses Z instead of Z².

**8. B.** The ground-state ionization energy is proportional to Z². For Be³⁺, Z = 4, so IE = 16R_E, 16 times that of hydrogen (R_E).

**9. D.** IE = R_E Z²/n² = (2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸)(3²)/(2²) = 4.91 × 10⁻¹⁸ J. 1.96 × 10⁻¹⁷ J is the ionization energy from n = 1; 1.64 × 10⁻¹⁸ J uses Z instead of Z².

**10. A.** Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit (1925) explained the doublets seen in the high-resolution hydrogen spectrum by assuming the electron has a spin, which needs a fourth quantum number. The Schrödinger equation gives only three.

**11. C.** mₛ = +1/2 or −1/2, also called spin up and spin down (α and β).

**12. D.** If the electron did not spin there would be one beam; if it spun in any direction the beam would broaden. Two narrow beams show that the electron's spin (acting like a tiny bar magnet) has only two orientations.

**13. A.** A spinning electron behaves like a tiny bar magnet (a dipole). In a uniform field the forces on its two ends are equal and opposite and cancel, so the trajectory is not deflected. A non-uniform field gives a net force.

**14. D.** An electron in a hydrogen-like atom is completely characterized by four quantum numbers: n, l and mₗ for the orbital, plus mₛ for the spin.

**15. B.** In a polyelectron atom the electron–electron repulsions must be considered as well as the attraction to the nucleus. This cannot be solved exactly even for helium (the three-body problem), so approximate methods are used.

**16. C.** Hartree's SCF method assumes (1) each electron moves in an effective field representing the average environment created by the nucleus and all the other electrons, and (2) the field is spherically symmetric. The equations are solved by iteration until the field and orbitals are self-consistent.

**17. A.** Hartree orbitals keep the hydrogen angular shapes and the quantum numbers n, l, mₗ, but their radial properties and energies differ significantly because each electron feels an effective nuclear charge.

**18. D.** Electrons close to the nucleus shield the nuclear charge from outer electrons, so an outer electron feels Zeff = Z − S, smaller than the full charge Z. S is the shielding constant.

**19. B.** The larger n is, the farther the electron is from the nucleus, the less it penetrates the electron core, and the smaller its Zeff.

**20. A.** s orbitals penetrate the core best, then p, then d, so Zeff(ns) > Zeff(np) > Zeff(nd).

**21. C.** Looking only at the most probable distance would suggest Zeff(3s) < Zeff(3p) < Zeff(3d), the opposite of what is observed. The 3s and 3p radial distributions have extra maxima close to the nucleus, in the core region, so those electrons penetrate the core and feel more of the nuclear charge.

**22. D.** Eₙ,ₗ = −R_E Zeff²/n². Since Zeff(ns) > Zeff(np) > Zeff(nd), the energies increase E(ns) < E(np) < E(nd). The degeneracy found in hydrogen is lost.

**23. B.** IE = R_E Zeff²/n², so 5.39 = 13.6 × Zeff²/2². Zeff² = (5.39 × 4)/13.6 = 1.585, and Zeff = 1.26. That is much less than Z = 3 because the two 1s electrons shield the 2s electron. (0.630 leaves out n²; 1.59 is Zeff², not Zeff.)

**24. A.** Hydrogen has no other electron to shield it, so Zeff = 1.00. In helium each 1s electron partly shields the other from the 2+ nucleus, so Zeff is between 1 and 2.

**25. C.** Zeff for the valence electrons increases going across a period, because the added protons are only partly shielded by electrons added to the same shell.

**26. B.** For a given n, the ns, np and nd maxima lie at similar distances, so the electron density is concentrated in concentric shells by n (1s; 2s 2p; 3s 3p for argon). The valence (n = 3) density is largely outside the core (n = 1 and n = 2).

**27. C.** 4s has n + l = 4 + 0 = 4; 3d has 3 + 2 = 5. Lower n + l fills first, so 4s is occupied before 3d (K is [Ar]4s¹).

**28. B.** For two subshells with the same n + l, the one with the lower n is completed first, so 4f (n = 4) fills before 5d (n = 5).

**29. A.** The filling order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p.

**30. B.** Both have n + l = 4, so the one with the lower n (3p) is completed first. That is why argon (3p⁶) comes before potassium (4s¹).

**31. D.** A period begins with the occupation of ns and ends with the completion of np (e.g. period 2 runs from Li, 2s¹, to Ne, 2p⁶).

**32. A.** The Pauli exclusion principle: no two electrons in an atom can have the same four quantum numbers. Equivalently, an orbital can hold only two electrons, and they must have opposite spins. B is the Aufbau principle and C is Hund's rule.

**33. D.** A shell holds 2n² electrons: 2(3²) = 18 (3s², 3p⁶, 3d¹⁰). 9 is the number of orbitals (n²).

**34. B.** A 3d electron has n = 3 and l = 2; mₗ can be −2 to +2 and mₛ must be ±1/2. (3, 1, …) is a 3p electron, mₗ = 3 is greater than l, and mₛ cannot be 0.

**35. C.** Hund's rule: if a subshell has more than one orbital, electrons are added to different orbitals with parallel spins rather than paired in one orbital. An atom tends to have as many parallel spins as possible.

**36. B.** Being in different orbitals keeps the electrons apart, and electrons with parallel spins spend more time apart than paired ones. Both minimize electron–electron repulsion. The energy difference between parallel and opposed spins is the exchange energy, a quantum mechanical effect.

**37. C.** N is 1s² 2s² 2p³. By Hund's rule the three 2p electrons go into separate orbitals with parallel spins. A and B pair electrons too early (and B has too many electrons); D does not have all spins parallel.

**38. B.** O is 1s² 2s² 2p⁴. Three 2p orbitals hold four electrons: one pair and two unpaired electrons with parallel spins.

**39. A.** Carbon has 6 electrons: 1s² 2s² 2p², with the two 2p electrons in separate orbitals with parallel spins (Hund's rule).

**40. D.** (1) An anion would need the extra electron in the next, much higher, level, which only becomes possible with more nuclear charge (the alkali metals). (2) A cation is hard to form because the large nuclear charge makes it hard to remove the outer electrons.
