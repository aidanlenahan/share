Shell\wd\All orbitals with the same value of n (e.g. 2s, 2px, 2py, 2pz).\ww\Subshell\wd\All orbitals with the same values of n and l (e.g. 2px, 2py, 2pz).\ww\Hydrogen orbital energies\wd\Determined only by n: E(2s) = E(2p); E(3s) = E(3p) = E(3d); E(4s) = E(4p) = E(4d) = E(4f).\ww\Average distance from the nucleus (hydrogen)\wd\For a given n, decreases in the sequence ns > np > nd.\ww\Node\wd\A region where the wave function changes sign and becomes zero; nodes may be angular (typically planes) or radial.\ww\Total number of nodes\wd\n − 1\ww\Number of angular nodes\wd\l\ww\Number of radial nodes\wd\n − l − 1\ww\Orbital energy and nodes\wd\The energy of an orbital correlates with the total number of nodes (as with a guitar string).\ww\Hydrogen-like atom energy\wd\Eₙ = −R_E Z²/n² for one-electron species such as He⁺, Li²⁺ and Be³⁺.\ww\Ionization energy of a hydrogen-like atom (1s)\wd\IE = Z²R_E: H = 1R_E, He⁺ = 4R_E, Li²⁺ = 9R_E, Be³⁺ = 16R_E; proportional to Z².\ww\Why a fourth quantum number was needed\wd\High-resolution lines of the hydrogen spectrum are doublets; Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit (1925) explained this by assuming the electron has a spin.\ww\Spin magnetic quantum number (mₛ)\wd\Can be +1/2 or −1/2, also called up and down, or α and β.\ww\Stern–Gerlach experiment\wd\A beam of silver atoms (one unpaired electron) passed through a non-uniform magnetic field split into two narrow beams, showing that the spinning electron has only two orientations.\ww\Stern–Gerlach: expected results without quantized spin\wd\No spin: a single beam; spin in any direction: a broadened beam.\ww\Why Stern–Gerlach needs a non-uniform field\wd\A spinning electron acts like a tiny bar magnet; in a uniform field the forces on the opposite ends of the dipole cancel, so there is no deflection.\ww\Quantum numbers that completely characterize an electron\wd\n, l, mₗ and mₛ.\ww\Polyelectron (many-electron) atom\wd\Any atom with more than one electron; electron–electron repulsions must be considered as well as electron–nucleus attraction.\ww\Three-body problem\wd\The Schrödinger equation cannot be solved exactly even for helium (nucleus + 2 electrons), so approximate methods are used.\ww\Self-consistent field (SCF) method (Hartree)\wd\Each electron moves in an effective field that averages the nucleus and all the other electrons; the field is spherically symmetric; each electron is described by a modified hydrogen-like orbital, and the equations are solved by iteration until self-consistent.\ww\Hartree orbitals vs hydrogen orbitals\wd\Same angular dependence and quantum numbers (n, l, mₗ), but different radial properties and energies due to the effective nuclear charge.\ww\Shielding\wd\Electrons in orbitals close to the nucleus shield the nuclear charge from the outer electrons.\ww\Effective nuclear charge (Zeff)\wd\Zeff = Z − S, the nuclear charge felt by an electron after shielding; S is the shielding constant.\ww\Zeff and n\wd\The greater n, the farther the electron is from the nucleus, the less it penetrates the core and the smaller Zeff: Zeff(1s) > Zeff(2s) > Zeff(3s).\ww\Zeff and l\wd\Zeff(ns) > Zeff(np) > Zeff(nd), because s orbitals penetrate the core best, then p, then d.\ww\Penetration\wd\The ability of an outer orbital to place electron density inside the core electrons, close to the nucleus.\ww\Why Zeff(3s) > Zeff(3p) > Zeff(3d)\wd\Although the most probable distance is 3s > 3p > 3d, the 3s and 3p orbitals have additional maxima close to the nucleus, within the core, so they penetrate the core more.\ww\Electron density shells\wd\For a given n, the ns, np and nd orbitals have maxima at similar distances, so electron density is concentrated in concentric shells by n; the valence density lies largely outside the core (e.g. argon).\ww\Orbital energy in a polyelectron atom\wd\Eₙ,ₗ = −R_E Zeff²/n²\ww\Energy order of ns, np, nd (polyelectron atom)\wd\E(ns) < E(np) < E(nd); the ns, np and nd orbitals of the same shell are no longer degenerate, unlike in hydrogen.\ww\Zeff of hydrogen\wd\1.00, since there is no other electron.\ww\Zeff of helium\wd\Between 1 and 2, since each electron partly shields the other.\ww\Zeff across a period\wd\Increases going across a period.\ww\Aufbau (building-up) principle\wd\The electron configuration is obtained by filling orbitals with electrons in order of increasing energy.\ww\n + l rule\wd\(1) Subshell energy increases with n + l; (2) for two subshells with the same n + l, the one with the lower n is completed first.\ww\Subshell filling order\wd\1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p.\ww\4s vs 3d filling\wd\4s (n + l = 4) is occupied before 3d (n + l = 5).\ww\Start and end of a period\wd\A period begins with occupation of ns and ends with completion of np.\ww\First d and f levels\wd\3d is the first d level (fills in period 4); 4f is the first f level (fills in period 6).\ww\Pauli exclusion principle\wd\No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers (n, l, mₗ, mₛ); an orbital holds at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins.\ww\Hund's rule\wd\If a subshell has more than one orbital, electrons are added to different orbitals with parallel spins rather than paired in one orbital; an atom tends to have as many parallel spins as possible.\ww\Why electrons occupy different orbitals\wd\It minimizes electron–electron repulsion.\ww\Why electrons prefer parallel spins\wd\Electrons with parallel spins spend more time apart, minimizing electron–electron repulsion.\ww\Exchange energy\wd\The energy difference between parallel and paired (opposed) spins; parallel spins are more stable. It is a quantum mechanical effect.\ww\Maximum electrons in a shell\wd\2n²\ww\[He] and [Ne]\wd\Symbols for the filled 1s² core and the filled 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ core (the core of the third row).\ww\Configurations H to Ne\wd\H 1s¹; He 1s²; Li [He]2s¹; Be [He]2s²; B [He]2s²2p¹; C [He]2s²2p²; N [He]2s²2p³; O [He]2s²2p⁴; F [He]2s²2p⁵; Ne [He]2s²2p⁶.\ww\Unpaired electrons B to Ne\wd\B 1; C 2; N 3; O 2; F 1; Ne 0.\ww\Inertness of the noble gases\wd\(1) Anions don't form easily because the next level is too high in energy; (2) cations don't form easily because the high nuclear charge makes it hard to remove the outer electrons.
