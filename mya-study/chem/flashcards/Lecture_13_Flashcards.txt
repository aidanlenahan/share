Atomic orbital\wd\A wave function that is a solution of the Schrödinger equation for an atom; called an orbital to distinguish it from the orbits of the Bohr theory.\ww\Hydrogen-like ion\wd\An ion with only one electron (e.g. He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺); its Schrödinger solutions are like those of hydrogen.\ww\Orbital energy levels (one-electron atom)\wd\Eₙ = −R_E Z²/n², n = 1, 2, 3, …; R_E = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.\ww\Schrödinger vs Bohr energies\wd\The energies are identical to Bohr's levels, but quantization is a natural consequence of the Schrödinger equation rather than an arbitrary assumption about angular momentum.\ww\Standing-wave condition for an orbital\wd\A whole number of wavelengths must fit into the circumference: 2πr = nλ, n = 1, 2, 3, …; fractional wavelengths interfere and destroy the wave.\ww\Principal quantum number (n)\wd\n = 1, 2, 3, …; determines the size and energy of the orbital.\ww\Radial and angular wave functions\wd\Ψₙₗₘₗ(r, θ, φ) = Rₙₗ(r) · Yₗₘₗ(θ, φ); the radial part depends on distance r, the angular part on the angles θ and φ (spherical polar coordinates).\ww\Quantum numbers for an orbital vs an energy\wd\The energy of a hydrogen orbital needs only n; the orbital (wave function) needs three quantum numbers: n, l, mₗ. A Bohr orbit has only one (n).\ww\Orbital angular momentum quantum number (l)\wd\l = 0, 1, …, (n − 1); determines the shape of the orbital; there are n types of orbitals for principal quantum number n. Also called the azimuthal quantum number.\ww\Magnetic quantum number (mₗ)\wd\mₗ = l, l − 1, …, 0, …, −l; determines the orientation of the orbital; there are (2l + 1) orbitals for a given l.\ww\Total number of orbitals in a shell\wd\n²\ww\Number of orbitals in a subshell\wd\2l + 1\ww\Number of subshells in a shell\wd\n\ww\Magnitude of orbital angular momentum\wd\|L| = √[l(l + 1)] (h/2π)\ww\z-component of orbital angular momentum\wd\mₗ(h/2π); only certain orientations of the angular momentum are permitted (its orientation about the z-axis is quantized).\ww\Angular momentum vector on a cone\wd\The magnitude and z-component of L are fixed, but the x- and y-components are indefinite, so the vector lies somewhere on the surface of a cone.\ww\Orbital letters\wd\l = 0: s; l = 1: p; l = 2: d; l = 3: f (beyond f, labels continue alphabetically: g, h, …).\ww\Origin of s, p, d, f\wd\Historical labels from spectral series: sharp, principal, diffuse and fundamental.\ww\Energy of hydrogen orbitals within a shell\wd\Depends only on n, so orbitals in the same shell are degenerate: E(2s) = E(2p); E(3s) = E(3p) = E(3d).\ww\Why orbital shapes matter\wd\The size, shape and orientation of orbitals determine bonding in molecules and hence their shapes.\ww\s orbital shape\wd\Spherically symmetric, because Ψ depends only on r and not on θ or φ.\ww\s orbitals at the nucleus\wd\All s orbitals have a non-zero value of Ψ at the nucleus.\ww\Radial nodes of s orbitals\wd\The number of radial nodes increases with n: 1s has 0, 2s has 1, 3s has 2.\ww\Node (orbital)\wd\A place where the wave function passes through zero and changes sign.\ww\Size of an atom\wd\Since the wave function stretches out to infinity, an atom has no boundary; its size is shown by a boundary surface inside which the electron has a specific probability (typically 90%) of being found.\ww\Ψ² vs radial probability\wd\Ψ² is the probability density at a point; the radial probability distribution is the probability of finding the electron in a thin spherical shell at distance r.\ww\Radial distribution function\wd\4πr² × R²(r), where R(r) is the radial wave function.\ww\1s radial distribution maximum\wd\The most probable distance of a 1s electron from the nucleus is a₀, the Bohr radius (0.529 Å).\ww\1s: point of greatest probability vs most likely distance\wd\The single point of greatest probability (largest Ψ²) is at the nucleus, but the most likely distance is a₀.\ww\Radial distribution at the nucleus\wd\Zero, because the volume of the spherical shell (4πr² dr) is vanishingly small at r = 0.\ww\Radial distribution and n\wd\The distance of maximum probability increases with n, and the energy increases as the number of radial nodes increases.\ww\p orbitals: Ψ at the nucleus\wd\Zero (unlike s orbitals).\ww\First p orbitals\wd\p orbitals first occur at n = 2; there is no 1p orbital.\ww\p orbital shape\wd\Dumbbell-shaped, with two lobes in which Ψ has opposite signs, separated by an angular node; shown with + and − or with different shading.\ww\Number of p orbitals per subshell\wd\Three, corresponding to mₗ = +1, 0, −1.\ww\px, py, pz\wd\An equivalent set of p orbitals, made from the mₗ = +1, 0, −1 solutions, that lie along the three perpendicular (orthogonal) axes.\ww\d orbitals\wd\First occur at n = 3 (3d); five orbitals (mₗ = +2, +1, 0, −1, −2); each has two angular nodes (one has a conical node, the doughnut); Ψ is zero at the nucleus; important for the transition metals.\ww\d orbital vs p orbital\wd\A d orbital looks like a p orbital sliced in two with opposite phases, i.e. it has an extra node.\ww\f orbitals\wd\First occur at n = 4 (4f); seven orbitals (mₗ = +3 to −3); each has three angular nodes; Ψ is zero at the nucleus; important for the lanthanides and actinides.\ww\Number of s, p, d, f orbitals\wd\s: 1; p: 3; d: 5; f: 7.
