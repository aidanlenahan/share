Electromagnetic radiation\wd\Radiant energy that travels through space as oscillating electric and magnetic fields in planes perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation; all forms travel at the speed of light in a vacuum.\ww\Wavelength (λ)\wd\The distance between two consecutive peaks or troughs in a wave; expressed in meters in λν = c.\ww\Frequency (ν)\wd\The number of waves (cycles) per second that pass a given point in space; unit s⁻¹.\ww\Hertz (Hz)\wd\The SI unit of frequency, cycles per second; since cycles is understood, 1 Hz = 1 s⁻¹.\ww\λν = c\wd\Relationship between wavelength and frequency for electromagnetic radiation; wavelength and frequency are inversely related (λ ∝ 1/ν).\ww\Speed of light (c)\wd\A defined quantity with the exact value 2.99792458 × 10⁸ m/s (2.9979 × 10⁸ m/s in calculations).\ww\Wavelength–frequency trend\wd\Short-wavelength radiation has high frequency; long-wavelength radiation has low frequency.\ww\Electromagnetic spectrum order (increasing wavelength)\wd\Gamma rays, X rays, ultraviolet, visible, infrared, microwaves, radio waves; frequency and photon energy decrease in the same order.\ww\Blackbody radiation\wd\Radiation that originates only from the thermal energy of a heated body, not reflected radiation, and does not depend on the material of the object.\ww\Blackbody radiation profile trend\wd\As temperature increases, the intensity maximum shifts to shorter wavelengths, matching the change from a red to a white glow as iron is heated.\ww\Ultraviolet catastrophe\wd\The classical prediction (assuming matter can absorb or emit any quantity of energy) that blackbody radiation has no maximum and goes to infinite intensity at very short wavelengths.\ww\Planck's constant (h)\wd\6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, equivalently 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ kg·m²/s.\ww\ΔE = nhν (Planck)\wd\Energy can be gained or lost only in whole-number multiples of hν, where n is an integer (1, 2, 3, …), h is Planck's constant and ν is the frequency of radiation absorbed or emitted.\ww\Quantized energy\wd\Energy that can be transferred only in discrete units of size hν, rather than continuously; gives energy particulate properties.\ww\Quantum\wd\A small packet of energy of size hν; a system can transfer energy only in whole quanta.\ww\Photon\wd\A particle (packet) of electromagnetic radiation, proposed by Einstein; energy of each photon is E = hν = hc/λ.\ww\E_photon = hν = hc/λ\wd\Energy of a photon, where h is Planck's constant, ν is frequency, c is the speed of light, and λ is wavelength.\ww\Photon energy trends\wd\Photon energy is directly proportional to frequency and inversely proportional to wavelength.\ww\Photoelectric effect\wd\The emission of electrons from the surface of a metal when light strikes it; explained by Einstein using quantized light (photons).\ww\Threshold frequency (ν₀)\wd\The minimum frequency of light below which no electrons are emitted from a given metal, regardless of light intensity.\ww\E₀ = hν₀\wd\The minimum energy required to remove an electron from a metal's surface, set by the threshold frequency.\ww\Photoelectric effect: light intensity\wd\Above ν₀, greater intensity means more photons and so more electrons emitted; below ν₀, no electrons are emitted at any intensity.\ww\Photoelectric effect: frequency\wd\Above ν₀, the kinetic energy of emitted electrons increases linearly with the frequency of the light.\ww\Kinetic energy of photoelectrons\wd\KE = ½mv² = hν − hν₀: the photon energy in excess of that required to remove the electron is given to it as kinetic energy.\ww\E = mc²\wd\Einstein's equation (1905) showing that mass is a form of energy; when a system loses energy it also loses mass. Rearranged, m = E/c² gives the mass associated with a quantity of energy.\ww\Photon mass and momentum\wd\Photons have no rest mass (mass only in a relativistic sense) but do have momentum, confirmed by Compton's X-ray–electron collision experiments; photons are also affected by gravity.\ww\Dual nature of light\wd\Electromagnetic radiation exhibits both wave properties and particulate properties.\ww\de Broglie's equation\wd\λ = h/mv: the wavelength associated with a particle of mass m moving at velocity v (momentum mv).\ww\de Broglie wavelength vs momentum\wd\Wavelength is inversely proportional to momentum: an electron's wavelength (about 10⁻¹⁰ m at 10⁷ m/s) is comparable to atomic spacing in crystals, while a ball's is far too small to observe.\ww\Diffraction\wd\Scattering of light (or other waves) from a regular array of points or lines, such as a grating or the atoms or ions in a crystal; can be explained only in terms of waves.\ww\Diffraction pattern\wd\The pattern of bright areas and dark spots produced when scattered radiation interferes, e.g., X rays scattered by an NaCl crystal.\ww\Constructive interference\wd\Waves in phase (peaks match) combine to produce a bright area in a diffraction pattern.\ww\Destructive interference\wd\Waves out of phase (peaks of one coincide with troughs of another) cancel to produce a dark spot in a diffraction pattern.\ww\Diffraction efficiency condition\wd\Diffraction occurs most efficiently when the spacing between scattering points is about the same as the wavelength.\ww\Davisson–Germer experiment (1927)\wd\A beam of electrons directed at a nickel crystal produced a diffraction pattern, verifying de Broglie's relationship and showing electrons have wave properties.\ww\Wave–particle duality of matter\wd\All matter exhibits both particulate and wave properties: large objects behave mainly as particles, photons mainly as waves, and electrons show both.\ww\Excited atom\wd\An atom that contains excess energy, which it can release by emitting light.\ww\Emission spectrum\wd\The wavelengths of light emitted by excited atoms as they release excess energy.\ww\Continuous spectrum\wd\A spectrum containing all wavelengths of visible light, such as white light through a prism or a rainbow.\ww\Line spectrum\wd\A spectrum containing only a few discrete wavelengths, such as the hydrogen emission spectrum.\ww\Significance of hydrogen's line spectrum\wd\Only certain energies are allowed for the electron in the hydrogen atom; its energy is quantized. If any energy were allowed, the spectrum would be continuous.\ww\Emission and energy levels\wd\Each line in an emission spectrum comes from a change between two discrete energy levels; the energy change determines the wavelength by ΔE = hc/λ.\ww\Bohr model\wd\Bohr's 1913 quantum model of the hydrogen atom in which the electron moves around the nucleus only in certain allowed circular orbits.\ww\Classical atom problem\wd\Classical physics predicts that an accelerating (orbiting) electron should radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus, which is inconsistent with stable atoms.\ww\Bohr's key assumptions\wd\The electron exists only in stationary, nonradiating orbits, and its angular momentum occurs only in certain increments.\ww\Angular momentum\wd\The product of mass, velocity, and orbital radius.\ww\Bohr energy levels (Equation 12.1)\wd\E = −2.178 × 10⁻¹⁸ J (Z²/n²), where n is an integer (larger n means larger orbit radius) and Z is the atomic number (Z = 1 for hydrogen).\ww\Negative sign in Bohr energy\wd\A bound electron has lower energy than at infinite distance (n = ∞), where there is no interaction and E = 0; every orbit energy is negative relative to this reference.\ww\Zero-energy reference state\wd\The electron at an infinite distance from the nucleus (n = ∞), where E = 0.\ww\Ground state\wd\The lowest possible energy state of an atom (n = 1 for hydrogen).\ww\Binding vs n\wd\Lower n means more negative energy and a more tightly bound electron; as the electron is brought closer to the nucleus, energy is released.\ww\One-electron species\wd\Equation 12.1 applies to any species with one electron: H (Z = 1), He⁺ (Z = 2), Li²⁺ (Z = 3), Be³⁺ (Z = 4), etc.\ww\ΔE for a transition (Equation 12.2)\wd\ΔE = E_final − E_initial = −2.178 × 10⁻¹⁸ J (1/n_final² − 1/n_initial²) for hydrogen; for other one-electron species multiply by Z² (from Equation 12.1).\ww\Sign of ΔE\wd\Negative ΔE means the atom lost energy (photon emitted, electron falls to lower n); positive ΔE means energy was absorbed (electron moves to higher n).\ww\Wavelength of an emitted or absorbed photon\wd\λ = hc/ΔE, using the absolute value of ΔE, since a negative wavelength is physically meaningless.\ww\Ionization from the ground state of hydrogen\wd\Removing the electron means n_final = ∞; the minimum energy required is 2.178 × 10⁻¹⁸ J per atom.\ww\Visible hydrogen emission lines\wd\Correspond to transitions from higher levels down to the n = 2 level.\ww\Limitations of the Bohr model\wd\It fits hydrogen's energy levels but does not work for other atoms and is fundamentally incorrect; electrons do not move in circular orbits.
