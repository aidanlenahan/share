the 3d–4s gap is then too large for moving electrons to 4s to pay off in reduced repulsion.\ww\Neutral metal atoms vs cations: 3d and 4s\wd\In neutral atoms 3d and 4s are close in energy, so 3dⁿ⁻¹4s¹ or 3dⁿ⁻²4s² can be favored; in cations 3d is well below 4s, so 3dⁿ is favored.\ww\Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ configurations\wd\Fe²⁺ [Ar]3d⁶; Fe³⁺ [Ar]3d⁵.\ww\Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺ configurations\wd\Cu⁺ [Ar]3d¹⁰; Cu²⁺ [Ar]3d⁹.\ww\Mn²⁺ configuration\wd\[Ar]3d⁵; each orbital holds at most two electrons with opposite spins (mₛ = ±1/2).\ww\Atomic radius down a group\wd\Increases: the outer shell has a larger principal quantum number (compare rₙ = n²a₀/Z), and the Pauli principle keeps added electrons out of the core region.\ww\Atomic radius across a period\wd\Decreases from left to right, because Zeff increases.\ww\First ionization energy across a period\wd\Generally increases, due to increasing Zeff and decreasing atomic radius.\ww\First ionization energy down a group\wd\Decreases, because the atomic radius increases while Zeff does not change significantly.\ww\Why IE(B) < IE(Be) and IE(Al) < IE(Mg)\wd\B and Al ionize from 2p and 3p orbitals, which penetrate less than ns orbitals, so Zeff and IE are lower.\ww\Why IE(O) < IE(N)\wd\Oxygen has a doubly occupied 2p orbital, and the extra repulsion makes an electron easier to remove.\ww\Electron affinity (lecture convention)\wd\ΔE for X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g);
