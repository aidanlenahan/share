Evidence that light behaves as a wave\wd\Light passing through two slits produces a diffraction (interference) pattern of alternating bright and dark lines.\ww\Constructive interference\wd\Waves that meet in phase (crest on crest) add together, giving a bright line in the two-slit pattern.\ww\Destructive interference\wd\Waves that meet out of phase (crest on trough) cancel, giving a dark region in the two-slit pattern.\ww\Traveling wave\wd\A wave that moves with a given speed (e.g. electromagnetic radiation, ocean waves); a marked segment of the medium only moves up and down while the wave moves along.\ww\Standing wave\wd\A wave (e.g. a plucked guitar string) whose crests, troughs and nodes stay at fixed positions; the amplitude at the fixed ends is zero.\ww\Node (standing wave)\wd\A point on a standing wave that undergoes no displacement at all.\ww\Standing waves: effect of more nodes\wd\The greater the number of nodes, the shorter the wavelength, the higher the frequency and the higher the energy of the standing wave.\ww\Standing waves and quantization\wd\Standing waves are an example of quantization: only certain wavelengths fit a string of fixed length.\ww\de Broglie's proposal (1924)\wd\Small particles of matter may at times display wave-like properties; the electron in a Bohr orbit can be pictured as a circular standing wave.\ww\de Broglie relationship\wd\λ = h/p = h/mv; the wavelength of a particle equals Planck's constant divided by its momentum.\ww\de Broglie condition for a stable orbit\wd\A whole number of wavelengths must fit into the circumference of the orbit: nλ = 2πr.\ww\Why only certain orbits are allowed (de Broglie)\wd\If a fractional number of wavelengths fit the orbit, the wave interferes with itself on successive cycles and is destroyed; only whole-number fits survive.\ww\Deriving λ = h/mv from the Bohr model\wd\nλ = 2πr combined with Bohr's mₑvr = n(h/2π) gives 2πr = n(h/mₑv), so λ = h/mₑv = h/p.\ww\Photon momentum\wd\p = h/λ; obtained by combining E = mc² (Einstein) with E = hν (Planck): hν = mc², so h/λ = mc = p.\ww\Evidence that electrons behave as particles\wd\Electrons are deflected by electric and magnetic fields, allowing their mass-to-charge ratio to be measured (J. J. Thomson).\ww\Evidence that electrons behave as waves\wd\Electrons are diffracted, like X-rays, by a nickel crystal (Davisson and Germer) or by aluminum foil (G. P. Thomson).\ww\Why electrons can be diffracted by a crystal\wd\The wavelength of accelerated electrons (like that of X-rays) is comparable to the spacing between the atoms.\ww\Why macroscopic objects show no wave behavior\wd\h is so small (6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s) that a large mass gives a wavelength too small to measure (a 0.145 kg baseball at 30 m/s has λ ≈ 1.5 × 10⁻³⁴ m); wave properties only appear for very small masses like electrons.\ww\Particles as waves: position\wd\Very small particles (atoms, electrons) cannot be described as mathematical points; they must be considered spread-out entities in space.\ww\Heisenberg uncertainty principle\wd\Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π; it is impossible to know simultaneously the exact position and the exact momentum of a particle.\ww\Δx and Δp\wd\Δx is the uncertainty in position; Δp is the uncertainty in momentum.\ww\When the uncertainty principle matters\wd\Only when the masses are as small as an electron; such particles become "fuzzy" and cannot be localized.\ww\Observing an electron with short-wavelength light\wd\Short wavelength means high frequency, energy and momentum; the photons disturb the electron's motion, making its momentum uncertain.\ww\Observing an electron with long-wavelength light\wd\Lower-energy photons disturb the electron's momentum less, but its position is known less accurately.\ww\Observing a baseball with a flashlight\wd\The ball is so massive that the photons do not change its position or momentum to any practical extent.\ww\Wave packet\wd\Combining waves of different wavelengths to localize a particle; because each wavelength corresponds to a different momentum, the momentum becomes more uncertain.\ww\Wave of a single wavelength\wd\Its momentum is known exactly (p = h/λ), but the wave is spread out in space, so the particle's position is unknown.\ww\Consequence of the uncertainty principle for atoms\wd\For an electron in a fixed energy level, only the probability of finding it at a given position can be known.\ww\Defect in the Bohr model revealed by the uncertainty principle\wd\The Bohr model gives the electron a definite position and momentum in a circular orbit, which the uncertainty principle forbids.\ww\Why Schrödinger used a wave equation\wd\If the electron is a wave, its position and movement in space must be described by a wave equation.\ww\Schrödinger equation\wd\HΨ = EΨ; a differential equation whose solutions are the wave functions (Ψ) and their energies (E). It cannot be derived and is regarded as a postulate.\ww\H in HΨ = EΨ\wd\The Hamiltonian operator.\ww\Ψ (wave function)\wd\The solution of the Schrödinger equation; it has no physical significance itself and may be positive or negative, but it contains the information to describe all properties of the wave.\ww\Ψ² (Born interpretation)\wd\The probability density for finding the electron at a given point (suggested by Max Born).\ww\Dimensions of standing waves\wd\Standing waves can be one-, two- or three-dimensional; a 3-D standing wave can describe an electron localized on an atom.\ww\Particle in a 1-D box: energy\wd\Eₙ = n²h²/8mL², n = 1, 2, 3, …; energy increases with n², and decreases with larger mass m or longer box length L.\ww\Particle in a 1-D box: wave function\wd\Ψₙ(x) = (2/L)^½ sin(nπx/L); the wave functions look like the vibrations of a guitar string.\ww\Particle in a box: quantum number n\wd\n = 1, 2, 3, …; n cannot be 0.\ww\Particle in a box: n = 1 probability\wd\Ψ² has its maximum value at the center of the box.\ww\Particle in a box: n = 2 probability\wd\Ψ² is zero at the center, so the particle has zero probability of being at the center in this level.\ww\Particle in a box: energy and nodes\wd\Energy increases with the number of nodes; level n has n − 1 nodes between the walls.\ww\Zero-point energy (ZPE)\wd\The lowest energy of a particle in a box (E₁) is not zero; zero energy would mean zero momentum known exactly, while the position is known to be in the box, violating the uncertainty principle. Particles in a potential well are never at rest.
